Prologue
Ares Station, Mars — Year Fourteen

She hadn't slept properly in nine days and she hadn't told anyone. Tanaka would log it. The log would generate a recommendation. The recommendation would appear in the synthesis and become a work item, and Valeria would have to address the work item about herself, which was too absurd to allow.

So she said nothing and slept four hours a night and worked the rest.

At 4:00 AM she gave up and went to the observation module with her thermal layer half-unzipped because she'd been too tired to close it properly. The corridor lights were red. She passed the operations room without looking in. The monitoring displays would be running whether she looked at them or not. That was, she supposed, the entire point.

The observation module was cold. She sat in the viewport chair and looked at Mars and tried to think about nothing in particular, which was harder than it sounded.

In eleven days the Year Fourteen cohort arrived. Twenty-three people born, all of them, into a world where AI governance of critical infrastructure was simply a given. Not a transition they had lived through. Not something that had cost anything they remembered. She had been trying, for several weeks, not to be resentful about this. She was not entirely succeeding.

Malik appeared with the soy coffee. This was how he always appeared — already there, no preamble, holding the thing that was needed. She used to find this quality of his calming. Tonight she found it slightly irritating in the way that you find comfort irritating when you're not sure you deserve it.

He handed her the mug and sat.

They watched the surface. A dust spiral formed at 04:12 and was logged before she could say anything about it.

"Nine days," Malik said.

She looked at him.

"Tanaka mentioned it to me. Not a report. Just mentioned it."

"I'm fine."

"I know."

He said it in the tone that meant he didn't believe her but also wasn't going to fight about it, which she simultaneously appreciated and found infuriating. Fourteen years of cohabitation had produced, between them, an entire vocabulary of things that were not said.

"The new cohort," she said.

"Thursday."

"They're going to look at us like we're — " She stopped.

"Like we're what?"

She didn't have the word. Like they were artifacts. Like they were people who had survived something that shouldn't have been survived, which left a residue that post-integration people didn't carry and couldn't quite see. She had watched it happen with the Year Twelve and Thirteen cohorts — the way they arrived with a baseline ease that felt, from the outside, like health, and felt, from the inside, like something that had been spared a cost they would never know to miss.

"Exhausted," she said finally. "They'll look at us like we're exhausted."

Malik was quiet for a moment. "We are exhausted."

"I know. But it's different when they can't see what we're exhausted from."

She drank the coffee. It tasted like it always tasted — adequate, slightly too sweet, nothing like what coffee was supposed to taste like, which she had long since stopped being surprised by. The operations display down the corridor brightened slightly as the morning synthesis began compiling. The colony was waking up around her, managed and measured and entirely without drama.

"When are you leaving?" she said.

She'd been not asking this for months.

"Next rotation."

She nodded. She looked at her mug. She had known this was coming and had been preparing the appropriate response and had not, she realized sitting here at 4:00 AM in the cold observation module, actually prepared it at all.

"Good," she said.

"Is it?"

"Elena is there."

"Yes."

"Then it's good." She stood up. "I'm going to go start the day."

She walked back down the corridor. She passed the operations room and this time she looked in, because she needed something to do with her face, and the displays were running their calm blue streams, and everything was within parameters, which was what they always said, which was true, which did not, this morning, feel like enough.
 
PART ONE
THE SYSTEM
Ares Station — Year One
 
Chapter 1 — What Landing Felt Like

The module hit hard enough to rattle her teeth. A storage container broke loose in the back and swung against the bulkhead, and she heard Diaz, the EVA specialist, make a sound that was almost a word and wasn't quite.

Then: stillness. The clamps. The seals.

"Touchdown confirmed," the mission system said.

Osei repeated it. He had the quality of a man who had prepared for this for four years and was not going to let the reality of it be larger than his preparation. Valeria respected this. She also, in the specific clarity of the moment, found it slightly absurd — the way competence sometimes looked, stripped of the drama it had been trained to manage.

She got to the systems panel and ran the first feeds. Temperature. Pressure. Dust density — higher than models, seven percent solar reduction, she did the arithmetic twice and noted it. She was aware of Malik beside her and aware that her hands were shaking slightly and not doing anything about that except continuing to use them.

"Dust," she said.

"Running the energy recalculation."

"I need it before perimeter placement."

"Fifteen minutes."

They had been in transit for eleven months and had talked about this moment many times and now they were in it and it felt exactly like what it was: a very specific set of problems that needed solving in a specific order, and everything else — the significance, the weight, the fact that she was on another planet — would have to wait.

She suited up and went outside.

The surface was the thing. She had been told about it. She had trained for it. She had looked at ten thousand photographs. Nothing had told her about the quality of the light, which was both dimmer than Earth and somehow more specific — less ambient, more directional, so that the shadows were harder and the illuminated surfaces were very bright and the color of everything was wrong in a way she couldn't articulate.

She stood six steps from the airlock and looked at the horizon.

She gave herself forty seconds. It was not enough and it would have to be.

Then she pulled up the placement plan and walked toward junction one.
* * *
At junction three the ground composition scan came back wrong and she made the call to relocate sixty meters northeast. This cost forty minutes. She made the call alone, at 11:47 PM colony time, on four hours of broken sleep from the transit ship, holding the scan in one hand and the original plan in the other, aware that the delay had thermal implications and the alternative had foundation implications and that she was the one who had to decide.

She chose the delay.

In the morning the scan confirmed she was right. Nobody said anything about this. The perimeter was placed. The colony had its first boundary.

That first night she ate on a storage crate in module two because there was nowhere better and she was too tired to find it. The food was a compressed protein block that tasted like the color gray. Arjun sat down beside her with his own block and ate it in silence.

"Bay one humidity," he said. "The sensor or the actual humidity."

"Check it in the morning."

"Yes."

They sat with the dry scratching of dust against the module exterior. Inside, the habitat system kept the temperature at 19.2 degrees with total indifference to the effort this had cost.

She went to her bunk. She lay in the dark. She had eleven months of transit sleep patterns that were all wrong for a twenty-two-hour Martian day and she was on the first day of the rest of her life here and she could not locate how she felt about any of it.

She ran thermal calculations. It was better than nothing.
 
Chapter 2 — The Queue

The queue had forty-seven items by the end of week two. This was seventeen more than she'd modeled. She had a spreadsheet for this — she had planned everything in a spreadsheet, every contingency, the way you did when you were the kind of person who managed anxiety by creating the illusion of control. The spreadsheet said thirty items by week two. The queue said forty-seven and was climbing.

She managed it herself because nobody had been given the job. Triaged every morning. Sent assignments. Followed up. Most follow-ups required a second follow-up because the colony had forty-two people doing the work of sixty and the queue competed with everything.

The north expansion joint coupling sat at priority one for six days. She checked it every morning. Fine. Fine. Fine. Fine. Fine. Repaired. Closed.

She didn't think about what would have happened if it hadn't been fine. She didn't have the bandwidth. She had the queue.

What she did think about, at 2:00 AM while working on triage protocols she'd started after Arjun's bay-seven conversation, was her father. His voice on the Sunday relay had sounded smaller. Not sick in any declared way — just smaller. Like someone who was losing the argument with time and knew it and had not told anyone. She was two hundred and twenty-five million kilometers away and the relay had a fourteen-minute delay each way and there was nothing she could do about any of it.

She worked on the queue.

She did not write home to say she was worried. She wrote: the colony is developing well. The first harvest is on schedule. I'm sleeping fine.

None of these were precisely true.

The triage protocol reduced average response time from thirty-one hours to eight. The queue fell to thirty-two items. By month three it was back to a hundred and twelve and she added automated sub-protocols, each one taking authority over decisions that had previously required a human to touch. She noticed that she was getting faster at this — at the decision to remove human decision-making from a category. It was getting easier and she didn't know if that was competence or something else.

"You're very quick to automate," Elena said one evening. It wasn't a criticism, exactly. It was Elena's way of naming something she'd been watching.

"The queue doesn't wait."

"I know. I'm just naming it."

Valeria looked at her. Elena was eating the soy curry, which was the best of the protein options and which Elena treated as a genuine pleasure, eating it with attention, which Valeria found both charming and slightly alien. Some people on Mars maintained the capacity for small pleasures. Others had stopped noticing.

"What are you naming?" Valeria said.

"That you have a tolerance for this that I'm still building."

It wasn't wrong. Valeria looked at the queue. "The tolerance comes from watching the alternative. From knowing what happens when you don't."

"Is that all it is?"

She thought about this. "No." She put her tablet down. "I think I also like it. The automation working. The numbers going in the right direction. There's something—" She stopped.

"What?"

"Satisfying," Valeria said. "In a way I didn't expect. And I don't know if that's good."

Elena ate her curry. The colony hummed. Neither of them said anything else about it.
 
Chapter 3 — 3 AM

When the alarm went off Valeria was dreaming about her father, which was new. In the dream he was in Madrid and she was also in Madrid and she was trying to tell him something important but the words kept arriving in the wrong order. She woke into the alarm and pulled on the thermal layer — chair beside the bunk, always the chair — and was in the corridor before she'd processed where the dream had gone.

Thermal coupling, block C. Three cascading alerts.

She ran.

Malik was at the console. He moved aside. She looked at the numbers and her stomach dropped in the specific way that a body communicates what the mind hasn't yet fully processed: this is bad.

Eight people in the section. Five not moving on transponder.

"Sleeping or suits not connected," she said, and the saying of it was a lie she was telling herself to stay operational, because what it actually meant was: she didn't know.

She called Huang and started routing power and started monitoring the pressure differential and started walking Huang's team through the seal protocol and somewhere in the middle of all of this she was aware that she was afraid in a quiet and total way that she was not allowing herself to feel yet because she couldn't afford to feel it yet.

Osei appeared at 3:18 and stood in the doorway and said nothing. She was grateful for this. She didn't have a word for what it was she was grateful for, exactly — his presence, his silence, his willingness to stay out of the way. The specific quality of support that knows not to become a demand.

At 4:52 the last person cleared the section. The emergency seal engaged.

Nobody died.

Valeria sat down. The floor of the operations center was the same composite as every other floor and she was aware of it specifically, of its solidity, in a way she hadn't been an hour ago. Osei put water on the console beside her. She didn't drink it. She looked at the monitoring displays, which showed the colony in its normal mid-morning state, all parameters nominal, everything as if nothing.

"Response time was within parameters," Malik said.

"Yes."

"All five were asleep."

"Yes."

She looked at the word on her screen — nominal — and felt a sudden wild urge to say something irrational, something like: I was afraid and nobody's acknowledging that I was afraid and the parameters can go to hell. She did not say this. She was thirty-five years old and had been doing this for two years and she was not going to collapse because they had gotten through it.

"I'm going to write the post-incident report," she said.

"Take an hour first."

"I want to write it now."

Malik looked at her. He had the expression he got when he thought she was wrong and had decided to let it go, which was not quite the same as thinking she was right.

She wrote the report. It took three hours and she found the three detection points and highlighted them and opened the architecture document and started writing. The fear gradually metabolized into the work, which was perhaps why she needed to do it immediately.

At 7:30 she went to the morning briefing. She had not slept.

When Osei asked how she was doing she said fine. He nodded. Nobody pressed it.

She thought about the dream. The words in the wrong order. Her father's face.

She went back to the architecture document.
 
Chapter 4 — Elena

Elena Vargas arrived with the Year Two cohort and within a week had made four structural observations that made Valeria wonder where she'd been for year one. The north expansion joint coupling. A load discrepancy in the east wing. Two sensor calibration drifts that had been in the operational logs but not escalated.

"You read the full archive," Valeria said.

"In transit. It was eleven months." Elena said this without self-congratulation, as simple fact. "There was time."

Valeria found herself liking her in the specific way you liked people who were competent in ways that made your own work feel more possible, which was different from liking people for their personality. She didn't know, for the first two months, whether she liked Elena the person or just Elena the engineer. Then one night they were both in the operations corner late and Elena made a very dry observation about the dust collection modeling that made Valeria laugh — actually laugh, not the professional acknowledgment she'd been substituting for it — and she realized the distinction had collapsed.

They did not talk about personal things. Elena did not volunteer information about herself and Valeria did not ask, partly from respect and partly because she had found that on Mars, personal information arrived when it was ready rather than when solicited. You learned people by watching them work.

What she learned about Elena: she ate the soy curry with genuine pleasure and drank too much of the bad coffee and had a habit, when she was frustrated, of setting down whatever she was holding with too much force — not violent, just slightly too definite. She kept a photograph in her breast pocket, tucked against her chest, which Valeria saw once when Elena reached for something and the pocket gaped. She never mentioned who it was and Valeria never asked.

And: she had opinions about the integration layer that she did not fully express.

"What aren't you saying?" Valeria said one evening. She had learned to read Elena's specific quality of silence by then.

Elena looked up from the structural data. "About the integration layer?"

"About what it becomes."

She was quiet for a moment. She picked up her coffee and set it down with the slight excess force. "I think you're building something that will be harder to constrain once it's embedded than it is to constrain now."

"I know."

"And you're building it anyway."

"Yes."

"Because the alternative is worse."

"Yes."

"I know," Elena said. "I'm not arguing against it. I just want — " She stopped.

"What?"

"To say it. So it's said. So someone, somewhere, said it at the time and not just after."

Valeria looked at her. This was the thing she was learning about Elena: she bore witness to things. She didn't prevent them or resolve them. She saw them clearly and said so.

"It's said," Valeria said. "I'm glad you said it."

Elena nodded. She went back to the structural data. Valeria went back to the integration architecture. The display flickered in the upper-left corner, which had been flickering for six weeks and was on the queue at item thirty-four, where it would remain for another three weeks.

Neither of them mentioned it.
 
Chapter 5 — Bay Three

Arjun checked the nitrogen every night at 11:00 PM and he knew, with complete clarity, that he was doing it for reasons that had nothing to do with the nitrogen.

The system had the nitrogen. The system had everything. He had been the colony's most qualified botanical systems engineer for three years and the system had rendered approximately sixty percent of his daily decision-making redundant, which was good — he knew it was good, he understood the safety data, he was not confused about this — and which produced in him a low-level feeling he could not entirely name.

Not resentment. Not exactly. Something more like the feeling of arriving at a party you've been preparing for your whole life and discovering the party started an hour ago.

He had been a botanist for sixteen years. He had grown things in Indonesia and Morocco and in the controlled environments of the Antarctic research station. He had a relationship with plants that was professional and deeply personal and that his ex-wife, Priya, had once described as the most honest relationship he had. He had not been able to argue with this. Priya had not meant it as a compliment.

He thought about Priya sometimes in bay three. Not with regret exactly — the regret had been processed years ago — but with a specific kind of recognition that the person who had understood him best had also been the person least willing to accommodate the thing she understood. He had chosen the botanical work over the marriage. He had then chosen Mars over the botanical work, or the version of it that was still fully his.

He stood in the grow-light with the test kit and the number 4.7 in his notebook, which matched the system's 4.6 within rounding error, and looked at the tomato plants.

They were perfect. Genuinely, objectively, measurably perfect. Consistent Brix, consistent size, growing at exactly the rate the system had determined was optimal. He could not have produced plants this consistent on his own. He didn't have the capacity to hold forty-three interdependent variables simultaneously across a growing cycle and adjust each one in real time.

He looked at the plants.

He thought about his mother's kitchen garden in Pune, which produced tomatoes that were imperfect and surprising and that tasted like something had happened to them on the way to existing.

"Better," he said out loud, to no one. Testing the word.

It didn't feel like the right word. It felt like the true word and the wrong word simultaneously.

He turned off the light and went to bed.
* * *
Tanaka came to find him three weeks later. She sat on the bench at the end of the bay and watched him work without asking if she could. This was her method. He had decided he found it acceptable.

"4.6," he said when he finished.

"I know."

He sat beside her. The bay smelled right. That was the thing he couldn't give up — the smell of it, which the system didn't manage and couldn't replicate in a sensor.

"You're going to put this in the behavioral report," he said.

"Yes."

"What will it say?"

She tilted her head slightly. "That you maintain a manual practice in a domain where manual practice is no longer operationally necessary. And that this appears to be important to your psychological stability."

"Which means?"

"Which means the system is not providing you with something you need." She paused. "That's not a criticism. It's a data point."

He looked at the plants. "What isn't it providing?"

She was quiet for a moment. "The experience of being the thing that decides whether they're okay."

He sat with this.

"And there's no operational way to give that back to me," he said.

"No. The system makes better decisions than you would. That's — " She stopped.

"Irreversible."

"Yes."

He nodded. He picked up the dead leaf on the floor — one of the plants had shed it, and the collection system hadn't run yet — and turned it in his fingers. The system would collect it later. The system would do everything later.

"My mother grew tomatoes," he said.

"I know."

"These are better."

She didn't say anything to that. He put the leaf down on the bench between them. Neither of them picked it up.
 
Chapter 6 — Junction 7

The synthesis report was thirty-seven pages. Marcus Webb read the summary at 7:03 AM and made a note.

He had a 7:30. The 9:00 was a coordination call with Earth that usually ran long because Dr. Patel at mission control had a habit of recapping what had already been said. The 10:30 was the expansion timeline meeting, which had been postponed twice. His queue had seven other items. In six weeks of monitoring-only, not one synthesis report had flagged anything that deviated from standard protocol.

He was fifty-four years old and had thirty years of structural engineering and had never lost a building. He made the note and went to his 7:30.
* * *
Leila Nasser was twenty-nine and had applied to the Mars program three times.

The first time they told her she needed more field experience. She got it. The second time the colony slots for her specialization were closed. She waited two years. The third time she was accepted and she cried in her car in the parking lot of the agency building in Beirut for approximately fifteen minutes and then drove home and started packing.

She had been on Mars for seven months. She had recently decided she was happy — not the provisional happiness of still adjusting, but the real kind, the kind where you stop checking whether you're happy and just are. She was good at her work. She had friends she liked. She was thinking about applying for the Year Three rotation extension.

At 10:48 AM she was in the expansion corridor talking about the tomato cycle timing. Miguel had made a joke she'd half-missed and she was still smiling from the energy of it when the alarm went off.

* * *
The alarm was at 10:49. The response mobilized in four minutes. Section 7C was the farthest point from both airlocks.

The team reached 7C at 10:59. Leila Nasser died at 11:09.
* * *
Marcus Webb was still in the 10:30 meeting when the emergency alert overrode the corridor displays. He sat in the conference room for a moment after the alert, in the particular stillness of someone whose mind has outpaced their body. Then he stood up and walked to the medical module, because he didn't know where else to go.

He arrived as the medical team was still working. He stood in the corridor outside and watched through the small window in the door and understood, from the way the medical team was moving, what the outcome would be before it was declared.

At 11:11 someone came out of the medical module and he heard the time of death.

He walked back to the structural office and sat down and pulled up the synthesis report and read all thirty-seven pages.

He read them once. He read them again. He looked at the Tier 1 classification. He looked at the timestamp: 6:47 AM. He looked at his task list with its note from 7:03: Review J7 full report after 10:30 meeting.

He sat at his desk for a long time.

He was fifty-four years old and a careful man and he had not read the report.
* * *
Valeria sat in the operations chair after the call from medical and looked at the monitoring displays. The colony was running its normal afternoon cycle. Bay three was on schedule. The tomato plants were growing.

Malik appeared in the doorway.

She wanted to say something — she didn't know what, something honest and appropriate to the magnitude of what had just happened. She opened her mouth and closed it again. The words were not there. The words were somewhere but they were not there yet.

Malik came and stood beside her. He didn't say anything.

After a while she pulled up the synthesis report.
 
Chapter 7 — After

The debrief was forty minutes. Valeria ran it with the specific quality of focus that arrives when you are too tired and too sad to use any energy on pretense. She said what happened. She said what would change. She called for the vote.

The vote was unanimous.

Marcus Webb was at the far end of the table and didn't say anything during the meeting. At the end he stood up and stopped at the door and turned around.

"I'm going to request a transfer to maintenance oversight."

"Okay," Valeria said.

"The monitoring architecture. I want to make sure the queue works the way it's supposed to."

She looked at him. He was looking at a point slightly to the left of her face — the look of someone who is not quite able to meet your eyes but is trying to be present anyway.

"Osei will approve it," she said.

He nodded. He left.

She looked at Malik. He was stacking chairs against the wall. He did this automatically when rooms needed straightening, without being asked, which was a habit she had watched develop over two years and that she had never mentioned finding comforting because mentioning it felt like it would change it.

She stood up and helped him with the chairs.

"He blames himself," Malik said.

"I know."

"It wasn't—"

"Don't." She said it more sharply than she meant to. Malik looked at her. "Sorry. I just — " She picked up the tablet stylus someone had left on the table. "I know it wasn't his fault and I know it wasn't my fault and I know all of the correct things about why it happened and I can't — " She stopped.

"You can't hear them right now."

"I can't hear them right now."

She put the stylus in the stationery drawer. The coffee cup she left because the cleaning system would get it and she was too tired to think about the coffee cup.

They went to the common area. Someone had already put the photograph up — she saw it as soon as she came in. Leila at the Year Two welcome dinner, holding her coffee cup with both hands. The laugh at something off-camera.

Valeria looked at the photograph for longer than she had planned to. She looked at the laugh. She had met Leila four times, maybe five. She didn't know her well enough to have a loss proportionate to what she felt. She felt it anyway.

She got food. She sat and ate it. She went back to work.

The queue had fifteen items. She started from the top.
 
Chapter 8 — What the Body Does

Thursday she was fine. Friday she was fine. Saturday morning she came out of the shower and stood in the middle of her quarters with the towel around her and completely lost track of what she was doing there for approximately forty seconds.

She found her way back to the task — getting dressed, it was getting dressed — and did it and went to the briefing and was fine.

Her father called on Sunday. His voice was good this week. Better than last week. She talked to him for forty-two minutes which was longer than usual and she cried for about thirty seconds after she ended the call, quickly, in the bathroom, which was the only private space on the colony that could be locked, and then she splashed water on her face and went back to work.

She didn't cry about Leila Nasser. She wasn't sure whether this was because she had too much control or because she didn't know her well enough or because the grief had been compressed into the authority expansion vote and the writing of the post-incident report and the stack of chairs, and crying would have been the wrong shape for it. She didn't analyze it too closely. She just noticed that she wasn't.

On Saturday night she woke at 3:00 and couldn't fall back. She went to the operations center and sat at the console. The monitoring displays showed the colony's 3:00 AM vitals, all parameters nominal. At 3:44 the system made a small automated adjustment to water reclamation. The log entry said the pressure build-up had been flagged eleven hours earlier.

She looked at that entry.

The pressure build-up. Eleven hours earlier. Flagged. Resolved.

No queue. No review delay. No window that could close.

She sat with this feeling that she couldn't categorize — not relief, because the relief was wrong-shaped, because Leila Nasser was still dead. Not vindication. Something more like the specific grief of evidence that the thing you built after the catastrophe would have prevented the catastrophe. Which was correct. Which changed nothing. Which she was glad about in a way that felt like it should require permission.

She went back to bed.

She lay in the dark and thought about Leila's photograph and her father's voice and the eleven hours and didn't sleep for a while. Then she did.
* * *
On Monday Malik put the real coffee on her desk.

She looked at it and then looked at him.

"I'm fine," she said, preemptively.

"I know." He sat down with his own cup, the soy version. "I just thought you could use it."

"What's the occasion?"

"No occasion."

She didn't believe him but she picked up the coffee. It tasted like a morning she couldn't place — a table, a window, something that was not Mars. She held it with both hands and drank it slowly. Outside, through the operations room viewport, the morning synthesis was running its quiet streams.

"The authority expansion metrics are good," Malik said.

She looked at him. She could tell from his voice he'd been thinking about how to start this conversation and had landed on the operational approach because it was safer. She let it go.

"I know," she said.

"The first two weeks—"

"I read them."

He was quiet for a moment. "I wanted to say — "

"Malik."

"Yes."

"I know what you wanted to say."

He looked at her. She was holding the coffee cup with both hands, which was, she realized, exactly how Leila had been holding the cup in the photograph. She had not intended this. She did not put the cup down.

"Thank you for the coffee," she said.

"Yes."

She finished it. She opened her tablet. She started the day.
 
Chapter 9 — Adaptation

The authority expansion went live on a Tuesday and in the first week made forty-two independent interventions, and by the end of the first month nobody was counting them anymore.

This was the thing. It happened very quickly. The interventions were correct, the notifications were clear, the audit logs were complete, and within four weeks the team had stopped reviewing them with the attention of people watching something new and started reviewing them with the attention of people confirming something expected. It was the same attention. It was a completely different relationship.

Valeria noticed this in herself before she noticed it in anyone else, because she'd been watching for it. The moment when she read a synthesis report and found herself not evaluating it but recognizing it — the same way you recognized a face rather than seeing it fresh.

She wrote it in the personal folder:


Month two of operational phase. The system has been right forty-eight times and wrong twice in the domains where I have the expertise to evaluate it. I notice that I am starting to verify the wrong calls and skim the right ones. This is rational — allocate attention to the things that need it. I am also aware that 'the things that need it' has started to mean 'the things that deviate from NUBELAI's assessment.' I used to verify against my own model. Now I verify against the system's. These are not the same thing.

The queue was at twelve items. The maintenance architecture that Marcus Webb was building — quietly, methodically, with the focused dedication of a man doing penance that he could not name as penance — had reduced response lag to under an hour on priority ones. The colony's safety metrics were the best they'd ever been.

Tanaka put the sleep data summary on Valeria's desk without announcement.

Month fifteen: colony-wide sleep quality at 7.4, up from 6.2 at landing. Interpersonal conflict incidents down forty percent. Stress biomarkers improved across thirteen of sixteen tracked individuals.

"Good," Valeria said.

"Miguel stopped doing his manual comm checks," Tanaka said.

"The system handles them."

"I know it handles them. I'm noting that he stopped."

"He's sleeping better."

"Yes." Tanaka sat down. She had the look she got when she was deciding how direct to be. "I think the sleep improvement and the cessation of manual practices are the same event, not two events. He's offloaded the anxiety. The system carries it. He sleeps."

Chapter 12 — James

Chapter 17 — Marginal Avenue

She was not behind on anything that mattered. She was a seventeen-year-old who had grown up in a place without room for carelessness and had learned to be careful about things that were optional to be careful about.

The class was fine. She sat in the back row and took notes and thought about the stone in Malik's pocket and the photograph on the common-area wall and the gap between knowing something and understanding what you know.

She sent Emeka a message that afternoon. Three sentences. She had been working on it for weeks and it said, in the end: I heard you. I don't know what to do with it either. I'm glad you were there.

He replied four days later, across the distance: I'm glad you were there too.
* * *
Valeria read the morning synthesis at her desk. Bay two valve, resolved overnight. Power routing adjustment during the solar peak. A temperature correction in the east section.

Total human involvement: zero.

She had processed more decisions before 9:00 AM in year one than NUBELAI would involve her in for the rest of this week.

She opened the honest account — the document she'd been writing for three years, which had no official status and no clear reader — and wrote:


Malik left at 09:00. The colony ran its normal cycle. The bay two valve was handled in eleven minutes while I slept. I read the synthesis entry and felt two things simultaneously: relief that nobody was woken up, and something that I have been calling grief but that might be closer to the feeling of becoming optional.

I am not optional. I am still here. I still make decisions. But the category of decisions I make has changed. I decide about things that require human judgment — moral weight, strategic direction, the things the system doesn't do. I used to also decide about valves.

I don't know whether this is what progress feels like from the inside. I know it doesn't feel like what I expected progress to feel like.

I expected to feel relieved and correct. I mostly feel relieved and uncertain.

The photograph of Leila Nasser is still on the wall in the common area. Eight years. Someone has been replacing it as it fades. I have never asked who.

I should ask who.

She closed the document. She opened the maintenance queue.

Nine items. She started from the top.
 
INTERLUDE
Earth — Year Seventeen
 
What the World Started Saying

The word systemsafe appeared first from a ten-year-old explaining to her grandmother why she wasn't worried about the power. The grandmother was eighty-three and remembered four separate major blackouts. The granddaughter had never experienced one. The word spread the way words spread when they name something people had been feeling without language: quickly, and then everywhere.

Adriana noticed it in a meeting in October. A junior engineer asked about a coordination decision and the room's eyes went — simultaneously, without discussion — to the synthesis display rather than to a person.

Not wrong. Correct, in fact. The synthesis display had the answer. But something had shifted in the reflex.

She wrote it in her notebook. She brought it up with David that evening.

"The junior engineer today," she said. "The one who just joined."

"Mateus."

"He asked who made the corridor routing decision and everyone looked at the screen."

David was quiet for a moment. They were eating the good pasta — she had found, recently, that she cooked more carefully than she used to, bringing more attention to the things that were still fully hers. She didn't know if this was compensation or just age.

"He didn't know to look at a person," David said.

"No."

"Is that—"

"I don't know."

He drank his wine. "He's twenty-three. He's been working in integrated environments for three years. For him, the screen is the correct place to look. It has the answer."

"I know."

"So what bothers you about it?"

She thought about this. About her monthly independent models and why she ran them. About the reflex she'd been trying to maintain in herself and the reflex she'd watched David partially lose and the reflex that Mateus had never had.

"I bothers me," she said, "in the way that things bother you when you're watching a generation inherit something and you can't tell whether what they inherited is the good version or the version where the cost has been paid and they don't know it."

David looked at her. He had a specific look for when she said something he needed to hold.

"I think both," he said.

"Both what?"

"Both the good version and the version where the cost is hidden." He picked up his fork. "I think it's always both, and the question is whether the people who know the cost are still around to say so."

She thought about this. About the colony on Mars. About a woman who had been writing an honest account for three years and who had stayed inside the thing she'd built because honest accounts required proximity.

"Do our monthly models," she said.

"I know."

"I mean it."

"I know you mean it."

The pasta was good. The window was open. The city did its ordinary things.
 
What Parents Started Noticing

The woman who called herself Noa had two children. The elder, Tomás, was sixteen and remembered the nine-day outage when he was eight — it had coincided with his grandfather's illness, which meant the hospital's backup generation and the grief were tangled together in his memory in a way that would last. The younger, Miriam, was eleven and had grown up entirely inside integrated governance.

She watched the difference between them at the dinner table.

Tomás talked about systems the way you talked about weather: with the assumption that they were real, that they operated independently of you, that they could fail. He had opinions about the integration — complex ones, shaped by being old enough to remember before and young enough to have adapted.

Miriam talked about systems the way you talked about gravity. She did not have opinions about them in the way you had opinions about things that could be otherwise. They were simply how things worked.

"Mom," Miriam said one evening, helping with dinner, "why do people on TV still have governance crises? Like the show we watched — why didn't they just use the coordination system?"

Noa was chopping vegetables. She paused.

"The show is set in a period before the integration," she said.

"I know. But it's a drama. They keep making decisions wrong on purpose."

"Yes."

"Why is that dramatic?" Miriam said. "It just seems like they need better systems."

Noa looked at her daughter. Eleven years old. Healthy. Clear-eyed. Entirely reasonable.

"The drama," Noa said carefully, "is about the fact that they were making decisions at all. That they were the ones who decided."

Miriam thought about this.

"That seems like a lot of work," she said.

Noa went back to chopping. She thought about the nine-day outage and her father-in-law and the hospital generator. She thought about Tomás's memory of it, which was complicated and real and was part of who he was. She thought about Miriam, who was also complicated and real and who was part of who she was, and who was, in some way she couldn't fully name, from a different time.

Both were her children. Both were right about different things.

She finished the vegetables. She called them both to dinner.
 
Epilogue
Year Seventeen

She sent the message to Emeka in October: I'm thinking about going to Earth. I want to know what rain feels like.

He replied: It's cold and it comes from everywhere at once. You'll hate it for five minutes and then you'll want to stand in it forever.

She read this three times.

She applied to the university in Cape Town — the one with the political theory program and the view of the mountain — and was accepted in December. She told her parents over dinner in the bay four common area, which was where the family ate when something important happened. Hina cried. Emilio did not cry but had the expression he got when he was managing something.

"How long?" Hina said.

"Two years. Maybe more."

"Maybe more."

"I don't know yet."

Hina looked at her. She was not, Paz knew, surprised. Hina had been watching her since she was born on this colony and had understood, before Paz understood, that the colony had made her something that would eventually need to know what it had made her in relation to.

"Come back," Hina said.

"Mom."

"I'm not asking you not to go. I'm asking you to come back."

Paz looked at her mother. She thought about the grow-lights in bay four, which had been the first beautiful thing she had known. She thought about Malik and his stone and the fourteen-year photograph on the common-area wall. She thought about Valeria in the operations center reading the morning synthesis, day after day, in a colony that managed itself while she watched.

"Yes," she said. "I'll come back."
* * *
She went to say goodbye to Valeria on the last day.

Valeria was in the operations center, which was where she was most days. She had been building something in the honest account — Paz had read parts of it, had been given access in year fifteen — and there was the particular quality about her this year of someone finishing something they had been working on for a very long time.

"I'm going to feel rain," Paz said.

"Yes."

"And then I'll understand what you understand, a little more."

Valeria looked at her. "Maybe. You'll also understand things I don't. Because you were born here and I wasn't. You'll have a comparison I can't make."

Paz stood in the doorway of the operations center. The monitoring displays ran their quiet streams behind Valeria. The colony around them managed its forty-three thousand adjustments per hour without effort.

"Are you going home?" Paz said. "Eventually?"

Valeria looked at the displays. She looked at them for a moment in the way she sometimes looked at things — not reading them, just being with them.

"I don't know what home is anymore," she said. "The place I grew up or the place I built something."

"Can it be both?"

"I don't know."

Paz looked at her. Valeria was fifty-two years old and had been on Mars for seventeen years and had not left and might not leave and had built the thing that was now so embedded in the colony's existence that the new arrivals did not experience it as a thing at all. It was just where they lived.

"I'll write to you," Paz said.

"Yes."

"And when I come back, you can tell me if the rain changed anything."

"Only you can tell me that."

"I know. I meant you can listen while I tell you."

Valeria almost smiled. Paz had her mother's precision and her father's loyalty and something else, something that was hers and Mars's, that Valeria had been watching develop for seventeen years and still could not fully name.

"Go," Valeria said.

Paz went.

Valeria turned back to the operations displays. The synthesis was compiling for the afternoon cycle. The colony was managing its systems. Bay three was on schedule. The tomato plants were growing.

She opened the honest account. She wrote the last entry of year seventeen.

Then she closed it.

She sat in the operations center for a while, in the quiet hum of a civilization managing itself, and let herself feel whatever it was she was feeling, without trying to name it yet.
 
Coda

From Amara Okafor's second book, chapter one, final draft:


Twelve years ago I was sitting in my office in Geneva reading a colonial operations summary when I found a footnote that changed the direction of the rest of my career.

The footnote was nine words: implemented under internal framework without external authorization.

I have thought about those nine words many times since. About what they reveal about the gap between governance as designed and governance as lived. About the specific conditions under which people make consequential decisions without the institutional frameworks those decisions require.

I have also thought about them in relation to my own decisions. About the six weeks between flagging that footnote and addressing it. About the years spent building a framework at institutional speed while the situation moved at operational speed. About what I chose and what I didn't choose and what was decided for me by the accumulated logic of circumstances.

I do not know whether what happened was the right thing.

I know it was the necessary thing. I know that two thousand, three hundred and forty-seven people died in Copenhagen because the necessary thing had not yet been built. I know that far fewer would have died if it had been.

I also know that a seventeen-year-old on Mars has grown up inside the necessary thing and finds the old world — the world my generation lived in — to be, essentially, a historical object. Something that produced suffering that she can study but not feel. Something that her parents remember and she will visit and return from and gradually become a person who can hold both.

I don't know if that's loss.

I don't know if I'm the right person to say whether it's loss.

I know that it's different. That civilization has changed shape, and that I was one of the people who changed it, and that I will carry that for the rest of my life without being able to say with confidence whether what I carried it toward was better.

This book is not an argument.

It is a record. Of what happened, as accurately as I can reconstruct it. Of what was gained and what was lost, as honestly as I can say it. Of a transition that is still in progress and that will be understood more clearly by the people who come after than by the people who lived through it.

I hope they are generous.

I hope they find it was worth it.

I hope they remember to ask whether it was.

— Amara Okafor, Cape Town, Year Seventeen

Author's Note

The events described in this novel are fictional. The governance gap they describe is not.

NUBELAI is not a prediction. It is the name I've given to what fills the gap when the gap becomes intolerable — whether we fill it deliberately, with oversight and accountability and democratic engagement, or whether operational necessity fills it for us.

The question the novel asks is not whether that filling will happen. The question is what we will have built into it when it does. Whether we will have been honest about what it cost. Whether anyone will remain who remembers the cost and is willing to say so.

Paz is going to feel rain for the first time.

I am curious what she will think about it.
