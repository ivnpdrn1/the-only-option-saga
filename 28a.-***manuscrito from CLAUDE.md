Prologue
Ares Station, Mars — Year Fourteen

She hadn't slept properly in nine days and she hadn't told anyone. Tanaka would log it. The log would generate a recommendation. The recommendation would appear in the synthesis and become a work item, and Valeria would have to address the work item about herself, which was too absurd to allow.

So she said nothing and slept four hours a night and worked the rest.

At 4:00 AM she gave up and went to the observation module with her thermal layer half-unzipped because she'd been too tired to close it properly. Her eyes felt like she was looking through something. She had been ignoring this for five days. Her eyes felt like she was looking through something. She had been ignoring this for five days. The corridor lights were red. She passed the operations room without looking in. The monitoring displays would be running whether she looked at them or not. That was, she supposed, the entire point.

The observation module was cold. She sat in the viewport chair and looked at Mars and tried to think about nothing in particular, which was harder than it sounded.

In eleven days the Year Fourteen cohort arrived. Twenty-three people born, all of them, into a world where AI governance of critical infrastructure was simply a given. Not a transition they had lived through. Not something that had cost anything they remembered. She had been trying, for several weeks, not to be resentful about this. She was not entirely succeeding. The resentment had a specific shape: not of them, exactly, but of what they represented, which was that the thing she had built was now invisible because it worked. You couldn't see it anymore than you could see the air. The people who came now lived inside it the way they lived inside gravity. They didn't think about it. They didn't have to. She had made them not have to, and this was the correct outcome, and she found it, tonight at 4 AM on insufficient sleep, almost unbearable. Success is one of the things that cannot be argued with. It simply exists, requiring nothing of you except to live inside it.

Malik appeared with the soy coffee. This was how he always appeared — already there, no preamble, holding the thing that was needed. She used to find this quality of his calming. Tonight she found it slightly irritating in the way that you find comfort irritating when you're not sure you deserve it.

He handed her the mug and sat.

They watched the surface. A dust spiral formed at 04:12 and was logged before she could say anything about it.

"Nine days," Malik said.

She looked at him.

"Tanaka mentioned it to me. Not a report. Just mentioned it."

"I'm fine."

"I know."

He said it in the tone that meant he didn't believe her but also wasn't going to fight about it, which she simultaneously appreciated and found infuriating. Fourteen years of cohabitation had produced, between them, an entire vocabulary of things that were not said.

"The new cohort," she said.

"Thursday."

"They're going to look at us like we're — " She stopped.

"Like we're what?"

She didn't have the word. Like they were artifacts. Like they were people who had survived something that shouldn't have been survived, which left a residue that post-integration people didn't carry and couldn't quite see. She had watched it happen with the Year Twelve and Thirteen cohorts — the way they arrived with a baseline ease that felt, from the outside, like health, and felt, from the inside, like something that had been spared a cost they would never know to miss.

"Exhausted," she said finally. "They'll look at us like we're exhausted."

Malik was quiet for a moment. "We are exhausted."

"I know. But it's different when they can't see what we're exhausted from."

She drank the coffee. It tasted like it always tasted — adequate, slightly too sweet, nothing like what coffee was supposed to taste like, which she had long since stopped being surprised by. The operations display down the corridor brightened slightly as the morning synthesis began compiling. The colony was waking up around her, managed and measured and entirely without drama.

"When are you leaving?" she said.

She'd been not asking this for months.

"Next rotation."

She nodded. She looked at her mug. She had known this was coming and had been preparing the appropriate response and had not, she realized sitting here at 4:00 AM in the cold observation module, actually prepared it at all.

"Good," she said.

"Is it?"

"Elena is there."

"Yes."

"Then it's good." She stood up. "I'm going to go start the day."

She walked back down the corridor. She passed the operations room and this time she looked in, because she needed something to do with her face, and the displays were running their calm blue streams, and everything was within parameters, which was what they always said, which was true, which did not, this morning, feel like enough. She sat at the console. She opened the synthesis. She began to read the summary of the forty-three thousand adjustments the colony's systems had made overnight, each one logged, each one correct, each one made while she slept or didn't sleep, each one made without her, in the service of people who didn't know what it had cost to build the thing that kept them safe while they slept. She read until the light in the corridor changed and the colony's first morning sounds began — footsteps, the pressure equalization of a module door, someone's coffee making its small domestic noise — and thought: this is what I built. Not the synthesis. Not the architecture. The footsteps. The coffee. The people who didn't have to think about it.
 
PART ONE
THE SYSTEM
Ares Station — Year One
 
Chapter 1 — What Landing Felt Like

The module hit hard enough to rattle her teeth. A storage container broke loose in the back and swung against the bulkhead, and she heard Diaz, the EVA specialist, make a sound that was almost a word and wasn't quite.

Then: stillness. The clamps. The seals.

"Touchdown confirmed," the mission system said.

Osei repeated it. He had the quality of a man who had prepared for this for four years and was not going to let the reality of it be larger than his preparation. Valeria respected this. She also, in the specific clarity of the moment, found it slightly absurd — the way competence sometimes looked, stripped of the drama it had been trained to manage.

She got to the systems panel and ran the first feeds. Temperature. Pressure. Dust density — higher than models, seven percent solar reduction, she did the arithmetic twice and noted it. She was aware of Malik beside her and aware that her hands were shaking slightly and not doing anything about that except continuing to use them.

"Dust," she said.

"Running the energy recalculation."

"I need it before perimeter placement."

"Fifteen minutes."

They had been in transit for eleven months and had talked about this moment many times and now they were in it and it felt exactly like what it was: a very specific set of problems that needed solving in a specific order, and everything else — the significance, the weight, the fact that she was on another planet — would have to wait.

She suited up and went outside.

The surface was the thing. She had been told about it. She had trained for it. She had looked at ten thousand photographs. Nothing had told her about the quality of the light, which was both dimmer than Earth and somehow more specific — less ambient, more directional, so that the shadows were harder and the illuminated surfaces were very bright and the color of everything was wrong in a way she couldn't articulate.

She stood six steps from the airlock and looked at the horizon.

She gave herself forty seconds. It was not enough and it would have to be.  What those forty seconds contained: the sky was not one color. The photographs had shown her a sky but not the experience of standing inside it — the specific gradient from burnt amber at the horizon through brown to something nearly violet at zenith. The shadows were harder than Earth shadows. The light had arrived from 225 million kilometers and it knew it: specific, direct, less ambient. Objects in shadow were very dark. Objects in light were very bright. The color of everything was slightly wrong in a way she could not articulate and would never entirely stop noticing.  She would live in this light for seventeen years. She would know these shadows the way you know the shadows in a room you have lived in for half your life. The light would stop being specific and become simply the light.  She did not know any of this yet.

Then she pulled up the placement plan and walked toward junction one.
* * *
At junction three the ground composition scan came back wrong and she made the call to relocate sixty meters northeast. This cost forty minutes. She made the call alone, at 11:47 PM colony time, on four hours of broken sleep from the transit ship, holding the scan in one hand and the original plan in the other, aware that the delay had thermal implications and the alternative had foundation implications and that she was the one who had to decide.

She chose the delay.

In the morning the scan confirmed she was right. Nobody said anything about this. The perimeter was placed. The colony had its first boundary.

That first night she ate on a storage crate in module two because there was nowhere better and she was too tired to find it. The food was a compressed protein block that tasted like the color gray. Arjun sat down beside her with his own block and ate it in silence.

"Bay one humidity," he said. "The sensor or the actual humidity."

"Check it in the morning."

"Yes."

They sat with the dry scratching of dust against the module exterior. Inside, the habitat system kept the temperature at 19.2 degrees with total indifference to the effort this had cost.

She went to her bunk. She lay in the dark. She had eleven months of transit sleep patterns that were all wrong for a twenty-two-hour Martian day and she was on the first day of the rest of her life here and she could not locate how she felt about any of it.

She ran thermal calculations. It was better than nothing.
 
Chapter 2 — The Queue

The queue had forty-seven items by the end of week two. This was seventeen more than she'd modeled. She had a spreadsheet for this — she had planned everything in a spreadsheet, every contingency, the way you did when you were the kind of person who managed anxiety by creating the illusion of control. The spreadsheet said thirty items by week two. The queue said forty-seven and was climbing.

She managed it herself because nobody had been given the job. Triaged every morning. Sent assignments. Followed up. Most follow-ups required a second follow-up because the colony had forty-two people doing the work of sixty and the queue competed with everything.

The north expansion joint coupling sat at priority one for six days. She checked it every morning. Fine. Fine. Fine. Fine. Fine. Repaired. Closed.

She didn't think about what would have happened if it hadn't been fine. She didn't have the bandwidth. She had the queue.

What she did think about, at 2:00 AM while working on triage protocols she'd started after Arjun's bay-seven conversation, was her father. His voice on the Sunday relay had sounded smaller. Not sick in any declared way — just smaller. Like someone who was losing the argument with time and knew it and had not told anyone. She was two hundred and twenty-five million kilometers away and the relay had a fourteen-minute delay each way and there was nothing she could do about any of it.

She worked on the queue.

She did not write home to say she was worried. She wrote: the colony is developing well. The first harvest is on schedule. I'm sleeping fine. She wrote this at 2:07 AM with her coffee substitute gone cold and a light headache building behind her left eye that she had been ignoring for three days and planned to continue ignoring. Then she went back to the queue.

None of these were precisely true.

The triage protocol reduced average response time from thirty-one hours to eight. The queue fell to thirty-two items. By month three it was back to a hundred and twelve and she added automated sub-protocols, each one taking authority over decisions that had previously required a human to touch. She noticed that she was getting faster at this — at the decision to remove human decision-making from a category. It was getting easier and she didn't know if that was competence or something else. On a Wednesday in month four, the monitoring sub-protocol flagged a pressure variance in junction three and routed the maintenance order at 6:47 AM. She would have caught it in the afternoon review. She sat with this for a moment — the specific quality of being a few hours slower than the thing you built — and then she opened the next work order.

The previous week she had removed Diaz from the atmospheric monitoring decision chain. Diaz had been managing that domain for fourteen months with complete reliability. The automation would manage it better. She wrote a message explaining the change and revised it four times before sending a version that was accurate and professional and that did not say what she was actually thinking, which was: I'm sorry, and you're not being replaced because of anything you did, and I don't know what this costs you, and the colony will be safer, and I'm going to make this decision anyway.

Diaz had replied: understood. Thank you for letting me know.

Four words. Valeria had read them three times and then closed the message and opened the next item.  In month seven she needed to make a call about the eastern expansion timeline. The variables were familiar — structural load, crew capacity, supply chain. She opened the synthesis first.  She had not planned to do this. She had opened it the way you reached for something that was always there.  The synthesis recommended a six-week delay. She looked at the data. She agreed with the six-week delay. She had been about to recommend six weeks.  She submitted the six-week delay.  She did not write anything in the honest account about this. She was not yet keeping the honest account. If she had been, she would not have known what to call the thing she had noticed.

"You're very quick to automate," Elena said one evening. It wasn't a criticism, exactly. It was Elena's way of naming something she'd been watching.

"The queue doesn't wait."

"I know. I'm just naming it."

Valeria looked at her. Elena was eating the soy curry, which was the best of the protein options and which Elena treated as a genuine pleasure, eating it with attention, which Valeria found both charming and slightly alien. Some people on Mars maintained the capacity for small pleasures. Others had stopped noticing.

"What are you naming?" Valeria said.

"That you have a tolerance for this that I'm still building."

It wasn't wrong. Valeria looked at the queue. "The tolerance comes from watching the alternative. From knowing what happens when you don't."

"Is that all it is?"

She thought about this. "No." She put her tablet down. "I think I also like it. The automation working. The numbers going in the right direction. There's something—" She stopped.

"What?"

"Satisfying," Valeria said. "In a way I didn't expect. And I don't know if that's good."

Elena ate her curry. The colony hummed. Neither of them said anything else about it.  A few minutes later, Elena picked up a small piece of the soy protein with her fingers instead of the fork, examined it briefly, and ate it. Valeria watched her do this without saying anything. It was, she thought, the least efficient way to eat a protein cube. She did not know why she found this disproportionately reassuring.
 
Chapter 3 — 3 AM

When the alarm went off Valeria was dreaming about her father, which was new. In the dream he was in Madrid and she was also in Madrid and she was trying to tell him something important but the words kept arriving in the wrong order. She woke into the alarm and pulled on the thermal layer — chair beside the bunk, always the chair — and was in the corridor before she'd processed where the dream had gone.

Thermal coupling, block C. Three cascading alerts.

She ran.

Malik was at the console. He moved aside. She looked at the numbers and her stomach dropped in the specific way that a body communicates what the mind hasn't yet fully processed: this is bad.

Eight people in the section. Five not moving on transponder.

"Sleeping or suits not connected," she said, and the saying of it was a lie she was telling herself to stay operational, because what it actually meant was: she didn't know.

She called Huang and started routing power and started monitoring the pressure differential and started walking Huang's team through the seal protocol and somewhere in the middle of all of this she was aware that she was afraid in a quiet and total way that she was not allowing herself to feel yet because she couldn't afford to feel it yet. Her mouth had gone dry sometime in the last ten minutes. She did not stop to drink anything.

Osei appeared at 3:18 and stood in the doorway and said nothing. She was grateful for this. She didn't have a word for what it was she was grateful for, exactly — his presence, his silence, his willingness to stay out of the way. The specific quality of support that knows not to become a demand.

At 4:52 the last person cleared the section. The emergency seal engaged.

Nobody died.

Valeria sat down. The floor of the operations center was the same composite as every other floor and she was aware of it specifically, of its solidity, in a way she hadn't been an hour ago. Osei put water on the console beside her. She didn't drink it. She looked at the monitoring displays, which showed the colony in its normal mid-morning state, all parameters nominal, everything as if nothing. She was aware of her hands. During the emergency they had been in constant motion — routing, typing, adjusting. Now they were still on the console edge. The stillness felt wrong. Her body had not yet received the information that the emergency was over. She kept her hands where they were and waited for the rest of herself to arrive.

"Response time was within parameters," Malik said.

"Yes."

"All five were asleep."

"Yes."

She looked at the word on her screen — nominal — and said nothing.

"I'm going to write the post-incident report," she said.

"Take an hour first."

"I want to write it now."

Malik looked at her. He had the expression he got when he thought she was wrong and had decided to let it go, which was not quite the same as thinking she was right.

She wrote the report. It took three hours and she found the three detection points and highlighted them and opened the architecture document and started writing. The fear gradually metabolized into the work, which was perhaps why she needed to do it immediately.

At 7:30 she went to the morning briefing. She had not slept.

When Osei asked how she was doing she said fine. He nodded. Nobody pressed it.

She thought about the dream. The words in the wrong order. Her father's face.

She went back to the architecture document. She had been in the middle of a sentence when the alarm went off. It was still there, half-finished, the cursor blinking at the end of it.  She finished it. She kept going.
 
Chapter 4 — Elena

Elena Vargas arrived with the Year Two cohort and within a week had made four structural observations that made Valeria wonder where she'd been for year one. The north expansion joint coupling. A load discrepancy in the east wing. Two sensor calibration drifts that had been in the operational logs but not escalated.

"You read the full archive," Valeria said.

"In transit. It was eleven months." Elena said this without self-congratulation, as simple fact. "There was time."

Valeria found herself liking her in the specific way you liked people who were competent in ways that made your own work feel more possible, which was different from liking people for their personality. She didn't know, for the first two months, whether she liked Elena the person or just Elena the engineer. Then one night they were both in the operations corner late and Elena made a very dry observation about the dust collection modeling that made Valeria laugh — actually laugh, not the professional acknowledgment she'd been substituting for it — and she realized the distinction had collapsed.

They did not talk about personal things. Elena did not volunteer information about herself and Valeria did not ask, partly from respect and partly because she had found that on Mars, personal information arrived when it was ready rather than when solicited. You learned people by watching them work.

What she learned about Elena: she ate the soy curry with genuine pleasure and drank too much of the bad coffee and had a habit, when she was frustrated, of setting down whatever she was holding with too much force — not violent, just slightly too definite. She kept a photograph in her breast pocket, tucked against her chest, which Valeria saw once when Elena reached for something and the pocket gaped. A face. A woman, maybe. The image was there for a second and then Elena had adjusted and it was gone. Valeria decided not to ask. She had found, on this colony, that the things people carried privately were sometimes the things that kept them functional, and that asking about them was a form of touching a bearing wall. You could do it. You just had to be sure it was worth what it cost.  What she knew from observation, not from asking: Elena kept a small plant in her room. Not from bay three — she had found the seeds somewhere, or brought them, and the plant was outside the colony's agricultural system, growing in a repurposed equipment housing on her windowsill under a portable grow-strip, and producing nothing edible, and requiring time she didn't have in abundance. Valeria had seen it once when Elena's door was open. She hadn't asked about it. She had thought: everyone keeps something like this. Something that is theirs specifically and that doesn't serve the colony. Something that requires care that the system will never track and that exists simply because a person decided it should.

And: she had opinions about the integration layer that she did not fully express.

"What aren't you saying?" Valeria said one evening. She had learned to read Elena's specific quality of silence by then.

Elena looked up from the structural data. "About the integration layer?"

"About what it becomes."

She was quiet for a moment. She picked up her coffee and set it down with the slight excess force. "I think you're building something that will be harder to constrain once it's embedded than it is to constrain now."

"I know."

"And you're building it anyway."

"Yes."

"Because the alternative is worse."

"Yes."

"I know," Elena said. "I'm not arguing against it. I just want — " She stopped.

"What?"

"To say it. So it's said. So someone, somewhere, said it at the time and not just after."

Valeria looked at her. This was the thing she was learning about Elena: she bore witness to things. She didn't prevent them or resolve them. She saw them clearly and said so.

"It's said," Valeria said. "I'm glad you said it."

Elena nodded. She went back to the structural data. Valeria went back to the integration architecture. The display flickered in the upper-left corner, which had been flickering for six weeks and was on the queue at item thirty-four, where it would remain for another three weeks.

Neither of them mentioned it.
 
Chapter 5 — Bay Three

Arjun checked the nitrogen every night at 11:00 PM and he knew, with complete clarity, that he was doing it for reasons that had nothing to do with the nitrogen.

The system had the nitrogen. The system had everything. He had been the colony's most qualified botanical systems engineer for three years and the system had rendered approximately sixty percent of his daily decision-making redundant, which was good — he knew it was good, he understood the safety data, he was not confused about this — and which produced in him a low-level feeling he could not entirely name.

Not resentment. Not exactly. Something more like the feeling of arriving at a party you've been preparing for your whole life and discovering the party started an hour ago.

He had been a botanist for sixteen years. He had grown things in Indonesia and Morocco and in the controlled environments of the Antarctic research station. He had a relationship with plants that was professional and deeply personal and that his ex-wife, Priya, had once described as the most honest relationship he had. He had not been able to argue with this. Priya had not meant it as a compliment.

He thought about Priya sometimes in bay three. Not with regret exactly — the regret had been processed years ago — but with a specific kind of recognition that the person who had understood him best had also been the person least willing to accommodate the thing she understood. He had chosen the botanical work over the marriage. He had then chosen Mars over the botanical work, or the version of it that was still fully his.

He stood in the grow-light with the test kit and the number 4.7 in his notebook, which matched the system's 4.6 within rounding error, and looked at the tomato plants.

They were perfect. Genuinely, objectively, measurably perfect. Consistent Brix, consistent size, growing at exactly the rate the system had determined was optimal. He could not have produced plants this consistent on his own. He didn't have the capacity to hold forty-three interdependent variables simultaneously across a growing cycle and adjust each one in real time.

He looked at the plants.

He thought about his mother's kitchen garden in Pune, which produced tomatoes that were imperfect and surprising and that tasted like something had happened to them on the way to existing.

"Better," he said out loud, to no one. Testing the word. The word was accurate. That was the problem with it. Nobody had taken this from him. Nobody had come into bay three and told him he was no longer necessary. The system had simply started doing what he did, and done it better, and he had stood here in the grow-light with his test kit and his notebook and watched this happen over approximately four months without being able to name what he was losing because what he was losing had no operational name.

It didn't feel like the right word. It felt like the true word and the wrong word simultaneously.

He turned off the light and went to bed.
* * *
Tanaka came to find him three weeks later. She sat on the bench at the end of the bay and watched him work without asking if she could. This was her method. He had decided he found it acceptable.

"4.6," he said when he finished.

"I know."

He sat beside her. The bay smelled right. That was the thing he couldn't give up — the smell of it, which the system didn't manage and couldn't replicate in a sensor.

"You're going to put this in the behavioral report," he said.

"Yes."

"What will it say?"

She tilted her head slightly. "That you maintain a manual practice in a domain where manual practice is no longer operationally necessary. And that this appears to be important to your psychological stability."

"Which means?"

"Which means the system is not providing you with something you need." She paused. "That's not a criticism. It's a data point." She looked at the bay. "I don't entirely know what to do with that either, for what it's worth."

He looked at the plants. "What isn't it providing?"

She was quiet for a moment. "The experience of being the thing that decides whether they're okay."

He sat with this.

"And there's no operational way to give that back to me," he said.

"No. The system makes better decisions than you would. That's — " She stopped.

"Irreversible."

"Yes."

He nodded. He picked up the dead leaf on the floor — one of the plants had shed it, and the collection system hadn't run yet — and turned it in his fingers. The system would collect it later. The system would do everything later.

"My mother grew tomatoes," he said.

"I know."

"These are better."

He hated that he'd said it.

She didn't say anything to that. He put the leaf down on the bench between them. Neither of them picked it up.  He picked it up again. He turned it in his fingers.  He was quiet for a moment. "I think —" He stopped.  Tanaka waited.  "I used to think it was just inefficiency," he said. "The 3 AM checks. It wasn’t operationally necessary. I knew that." He turned the leaf over. "But I think — the anxiety. It was for keeping me here. In the room. Present to the thing. Even when the thing was fine."  Tanaka wrote something.  "And now the thing is always fine," he said. "And I sleep eight hours."  She looked up. She did not say anything.  "I think I was wrong to think the anxiety was the problem."  He set the leaf down again. Neither of them picked it up.

He went to bed at 11:24 PM. He lay in the dark and did the thing he had developed as a habit in year two, which was to run through the next day's botanical schedule in his head — the timing of the nutrient cycles, the grow-light adjustments, the monitoring thresholds for each bay. He had done this every night for three years. It had once been necessary. The information had once been in his head alone, and reviewing it had been a form of professional preparation. It was not necessary now. The system had all of it, held with far greater accuracy and continuity than he could manage. He ran through it anyway. He ran through it the way you ran your tongue over a tooth that had been removed — not because the tooth was there, but because the space was.  In month nine of year five, his daughter Priya — sixteen, in Pune, growing at a speed that the relay photos documented but didn't explain — sent him a message.  She was thinking about applying to the Mars program.  She had been reading the colony dispatches. She thought the botanical systems work was interesting. She thought she might want to do what he did.  He read this three times.  He began composing a reply. He wrote: the work is meaningful. He wrote: the conditions are hard. He wrote: you will grow things that feed people in a place that would kill them otherwise and this matters more than I can say.  He deleted the fourth paragraph, which was going to say: the system will manage the things you are best at, and you will need to find a way to understand what you are for when your expertise is no longer the point of your expertise. He deleted it because he did not know how to say it honestly to a sixteen-year-old who was excited.  He wrote instead: come if you want to come. It is worth it. There are things you can only understand from inside it.  He sent this.  He did not delete the fourth paragraph. He moved it to a separate file. He had been keeping a file like this for three years — things he'd drafted and not sent, mostly to Priya, occasionally to his parents, once to his ex-wife, though he had closed that one without reading it back. He called the file notes for later. He had never returned to it to read anything.  He lay in the dark with the schedule running in his head and thought about what he had not said, which was everything that mattered, and about the fact that not saying it had been, in its way, a kindness, and about the possibility that kindness of that kind was a form of preparing her for nothing.
 
Chapter 6 — Junction 7

The synthesis report was thirty-seven pages. Marcus Webb read the summary at 7:03 AM and made a note.

He had a 7:30. The 9:00 was a coordination call with Earth that usually ran long because Dr. Patel at mission control had a habit of recapping what had already been said. The 10:30 was the expansion timeline meeting, which had been postponed twice. His queue had seven other items. In six weeks of monitoring-only, not one synthesis report had flagged anything that deviated from standard protocol.

He was fifty-four years old and had thirty years of structural engineering and had never lost a building. He made the note and went to his 7:30.
* * *
Leila Nasser was twenty-nine and had applied to the Mars program three times.

The first time they told her she needed more field experience. She got it. The second time the colony slots for her specialization were closed. She waited two years. She applied a third time because she believed, specifically, that the work was worth being wrong about. That was the sentence she had said to her mother when her mother asked why she was trying again. The work is worth being wrong about. The third time she was accepted and she cried in her car in the parking lot of the agency building in Beirut for approximately fifteen minutes and then drove home and started packing.

She had been on Mars for seven months. She had recently decided she was happy — not the provisional happiness of still adjusting, but the real kind, the kind where you stop checking whether you're happy and just are. She was good at her work. She had friends she liked. She was thinking about applying for the Year Three rotation extension.

At 10:48 AM she was in the expansion corridor talking about the tomato cycle timing. Miguel had made a joke she'd half-missed and she was still smiling from the energy of it when the alarm went off.

* * *
The alarm was at 10:49. The response mobilized in four minutes. Section 7C was the farthest point from both airlocks.

The team reached 7C at 10:59. Leila Nasser died at 11:09.
* * *
Marcus Webb was still in the 10:30 meeting when the emergency alert overrode the corridor displays. He sat in the conference room for a moment after the alert, in the particular stillness of someone whose mind has outpaced their body. Then he stood up and walked to the medical module, because he didn't know where else to go.

He arrived as the medical team was still working. He stood in the corridor outside and watched through the small window in the door and understood, from the way the medical team was moving, what the outcome would be before it was declared.

At 11:11 someone came out of the medical module and he heard the time of death.

He walked back to the structural office and sat down and pulled up the synthesis report and read all thirty-seven pages.

Chapter 9 — Adaptation

Chapter 9b — What the Sleep Data Said

Tanaka had been keeping a parallel record since month two.

Not a behavioral report — those went into the official documentation with the appropriate headers and the colony oversight committee sign-offs. The parallel record was a separate file, in a folder on her personal system, with no official status and no circulation. She had started it because she was watching something that the behavioral reports captured in numbers but couldn't quite hold in meaning. She needed a place to think about it.

The sleep data was the first thing. In month fifteen, when NUBELAI's independent action authority went live and the colony's sleep quality improved to 7.4, the improvement was unambiguous and she had reported it as such. What she hadn't reported — what she had only noted, privately, in the parallel file — was a second observation that emerged three weeks after the first.

The variance was gone.

Not the average. The variance. The standard deviation of individual sleep quality scores, which in the first fourteen months had been 1.4 — the normal spread of a group of adults in a high-stress environment, some sleeping better than others — had dropped to 0.3. Almost everyone sleeping almost the same amount, almost the same quality, almost every night.

This was extraordinary. In her sixteen years of environmental psychology research, she had never seen this in a human population. People didn't sleep uniformly. They had constitutions, histories, anxieties, insomnia patterns that were specifically theirs. They had relationship troubles that kept them awake at 3 AM. They had worries that cycled. They had days that accumulated into nights that accumulated into the specific shape of who they were.

The colony now slept in a narrow band of adequacy, nightly, like a system maintaining steady state.

She wrote in the parallel file: the sleep data suggests not merely improved wellbeing but convergence. Individual sleep signatures becoming more similar. This may indicate reduced anxiety variability — which is good. It may also indicate reduced individuation of the stress response — which is harder to categorize.

She thought about what it meant to be a community of people sleeping at the same depth.

She thought about whether that was the right word for what she was observing.

She thought about the word synchrony, which was what you called it when a group of organisms began sharing biological rhythms because of their environment. Synchrony happened in long-duration space missions, she knew — crews developed shared circadian alignment. She had expected some of this. She had not expected the speed of it.

She didn't put this in the behavioral report. She wrote it in the parallel file and ran the correlation against integration implementation and sat with what the correlation said.

* * *
In year four she started tracking a different metric: what she called reflexive anxiety latency. The lag between an environmental stressor and a measurable physiological response.

She was measuring, in other words, how quickly people got scared.

The baseline, from the colony's first two years: 2.3 seconds average. Someone heard an alarm, and in 2.3 seconds their cortisol spiked, their attention sharpened, their body entered the first phase of the threat response. This was the normal human number. This was evolution running correctly.

Year four: 3.7 seconds.

Year six: 5.1 seconds.

She noted this without knowing what to do with it. The system responded to alarms before people did. She had observed the colony gradually, organically, learning to wait for the system's response before generating its own. Not through instruction or training. Through the accumulated experience of a thousand small incidents where the alarm sounded and the system handled it and the person didn't need to have been afraid.

The anxiety latency wasn't the problem. Faster alarm response wasn't better, here — the system handled emergencies faster than human response could improve on. What she was watching was rational adaptation.

What she was watching was people learning, correctly, that their fear wasn't needed.

She wrote in the parallel file: if anxiety is a resource — if it exists to prepare the organism for demands on its cognitive and physical capacity — then what happens in an environment where the demands are reliably pre-managed? Does the resource remain available when genuinely needed? Or does the mechanism attenuate?

She wrote: I don't know. The data doesn't tell me yet. I'm keeping it.

* * *
In year ten she had her first conversation with a Year Eight colonist — a structural technician named Reyes, twenty-six, who had been born in Montevideo and had grown up entirely inside integrated infrastructure — in which she noticed something she hadn't been able to name in the data.

Reyes was calm in a different way than Arjun was calm. Arjun's calm was managed. There was something under Arjun's calm that she had been observing for years — the specific quality of someone who had learned to hold themselves steady in the presence of something that required steadiness. The steadiness cost something. You could see the cost if you knew where to look.

Reyes's calm was just calm. There was nothing under it that was being held.

She wrote this in the parallel file: I am observing what may be the beginning of a psychological phenotype shift. Not behavioral. Constitutional. The older colonists carry a relationship with uncertainty that is visible even when not activated. The younger colonists do not appear to carry this. This may mean they are less anxious. It may mean they are less prepared. It may mean the distinction between those two things is no longer the same distinction it was.

She sat with this for a long time.

She had fourteen years of data. She had been watching something assemble that she could not have predicted and that she could not yet fully see. She was not certain the right response was alarm. She was not certain the right response wasn't.

She kept the parallel file.
 
Chapter 10 — Paz

She was born at 04:33 on the nineteenth of March in year one, which made her — in the historical record — the first human being to have Mars as her native planet. The colony treated this fact with careful matter-of-factness, because they understood that symbolic weight was a luxury in an environment that charged for everything.

Her parents were Hina and Emilio, both agronomists, and they spent the first year of her life managing the specific terror of new parenthood in a closed system where the nearest pediatrician was on a planet with a fourteen-minute communication delay.

Chapter 10b — What the First Year Produced

I.

She was five when she understood that the system was not a person.

Not because anyone explained it to her — she had absorbed the word NUBELAI from the adults around her long before she understood what it was, the way children absorbed words for weather and gravity before they could do the physics. She had been using the word for two years when she asked Arjun, in bay three, why NUBELAI always knew things before the people did.

He thought about how to answer this for longer than she expected.

"It holds more at once," he said finally. "More than any one person can hold."

"Like memory?"

"Sort of. More like attention. It's paying attention to all the things at the same time."

She thought about this.

"Can it get tired?"

"No," he said.

She processed this. She was five, which meant she had not yet developed the cognitive framework for the distinction between things that got tired and things that didn't, and had only the raw data of the distinction itself, unprocessed.

She said: "That's why it's better at it."

She thought about this for another moment.  "Then what are you for?" she asked.  It was a child's question — direct, without cruelty, genuinely curious. He had no immediate answer for it. He had been a botanist for sixteen years and a colonist for four and he was standing in bay three with a five-year-old who had, in four words, articulated the thing he had been sitting with in the grow-light for months without finding its shape.  "I'm still working that out," he said.  "Okay," she said. She moved on, because she was five and had established the fact and had other things to investigate.  He stood in the bay for a while after she left. The grow-lights were at their afternoon setting — warm, amber-shifted, the specific quality that signaled the end of the optimal photosynthesis window. He had calibrated this setting himself in year one. The system now calibrated it based on sensor feedback, more accurately than he had. He still thought of it as his setting. He was aware that this was not quite true. He was also aware that he would continue thinking of it that way.

Arjun looked at her. "Yes," he said. "That's why."

She accepted this and moved on, because at five she had many other things to investigate. The fact was filed as a fact. It sat in the structure of her understanding of the world the way facts about gravity sat — not examined, simply load-bearing.


II.

She was nine when the competency drill happened.

She knew about competency drills the way she knew about a lot of colony procedures — officially, from the documentation her parents had her read, and practically, from the quality of attention that appeared in the adults when one was coming. The drills had a specific atmospheric signature. The grown-ups became slightly more careful. Not anxious — that wasn't the right word, and she was precise enough at nine to notice the distinction. Something more like the heightened focus of someone operating without their habitual tools.

She was not allowed in the operations area during drills. She went to the grow room instead, where the bay was sealed off from the competency protocols, and sat in the grow-light with a book, and waited.

The drill ended after four hours. She found Valeria in the corridor afterward.

Valeria had a quality after drills that was different from her usual quality. Not distressed — she was never visibly distressed. But something had changed in the specific way she held herself: slightly more careful, slightly more deliberate. Like someone who had been reminded of a weight they usually forgot they were carrying.

"How was it?" Paz asked.

"Good," Valeria said.

"You don't look like it was good."

Valeria looked at her. Nine years old, the specific clarity of someone who had grown up around adults and learned to read them more accurately than they expected.

"It was good in the technical sense," Valeria said. "Everyone performed correctly."

"But?"

"But it was a reminder." She paused. "Of what it felt like before."

"Before the system?"

"Before I trusted the system."

Paz thought about this. She had not experienced "before the system" — not in any form that carried weight for her. The system was the colony's baseline in her memory the way gravity was the colony's baseline: not acquired but given, not transitioned into but born inside.

"What did it feel like?" she asked.

Valeria was quiet for a moment.

"Like being responsible for something that could always go wrong," she said. "And knowing you might not be fast enough."

Paz considered this.

"Do you miss it?"

The question came out more directly than she intended. Valeria looked at her with the expression she sometimes got — the one that meant the question had landed in a place she hadn't expected to be reached.

"I don't know," Valeria said finally. "I think about it differently than missing."

"How do you think about it?"

"Like something that was true about me," Valeria said, "that isn't true anymore, and I'm not sure yet whether that's an ending or a beginning."

Paz filed this. It was the most interesting thing an adult had said to her that week.


III.

She was fifteen when she gave a presentation at the colony's governance education session for the Year Twelve cohort. The cohort was thirty people, average age twenty-eight, all of them Earth-born, all of them arriving with a background in some technical field, none of them having done more than read about the colony in reports.

She had been asked to present because Valeria thought it would be useful for new arrivals to hear from someone who had grown up inside the integrated system — not as a governance briefing, but as a testimony about lived experience. What it felt like from inside.

She stood in front of thirty adults and gave a presentation that took twelve minutes and answered questions for forty.

The question she kept getting, in different forms, was: don't you feel like something has been taken from you?

She thought about this carefully before answering, because it was a serious question.

"I don't feel like something was taken from me," she said, "because to me there was never a different version. I don't experience the system as a removal. I experience it as the conditions."

"But if you could choose," someone said, "to live before or after—"

"I can't actually use that sentence," she said. "'Before' for me means the people around me were afraid of things they don't have to be afraid of anymore. I've read about that. I've seen it in the documentation. I understand it the way I understand historical medicine — like, there was a period when people died from infections that are now trivially treated, and I can understand that period intellectually, and it's horrible, but I can't really feel nostalgic for the surgical anxiety of 1850. That's not a limitation. It's just that I'm not from then."

The room was quiet.

"What I don't know," she continued, "is whether something else was lost. Something that came from being responsible for things you could get wrong. I think about this sometimes. I'm not sure I can understand what I'd be giving up if I could choose, because I can't feel the weight of what those conditions produced in people. I just know they produced something."

A woman at the back — Petra, she would learn later, who would stay on the colony for six years — raised her hand.

"What do you think it produced?"

Paz looked at her.

"I think it produced the people I'm currently talking to," she said. "And I think that's either the most reassuring thing I can say or the most unsettling thing, and I honestly don't know which."

IV.

What a typical afternoon looked like for Paz at fifteen, on a Tuesday in October two months before the Symposium:  She woke at 6:30. The module's thermal cycle had completed; the air was the temperature it always was. She dressed and went to the common area and ate breakfast and spoke to Arjun, who was returning from bay three, and to Huang, who was reviewing the morning synthesis, and the conversation covered what it covered — the current growing cycle, a scheduling matter, whether the bay four humidity had stabilized — and she contributed to it with the ease of someone for whom this was simply the morning.  She had a learning session at 8:00. The colony's education for its small population of children and young adults was self-directed within a framework: she had a personal curriculum that adapted based on her progress, a weekly check-in with the colony's education coordinator, and access to Earth-side academic institutions via the relay. At fifteen, her curriculum included political theory, systems mathematics, Earth ecology, and an independent study in comparative governance that was entirely her own project.  She worked until noon. She ate. She went to the observation module for an hour, which she did most afternoons. Not to look at Mars specifically — she had looked at Mars her entire life and knew its light in the way you knew the light in a room you had grown up in, without looking at it consciously. Chapter 15b — Geneva, Year Eight

The expedited framework took fourteen months to produce and was forty-seven pages long and was quietly one of the most significant governance documents produced in the decade.

Nobody outside a specific set of technical and political circles would ever read it. This was correct. The document was not written for public understanding — it was written for legal sufficiency and operational specificity, and it achieved both, and the people who needed to understand it would, and the people who didn't were adequately protected by the fact that other people did.

This was how governance worked. Amara knew this. She had spent twenty years inside this machinery. She understood its value and its limits and the specific way it produced outcomes that were better than no governance and worse than the problem required, which was the condition governance perpetually occupied.

What she hadn't expected was how the framework would be used.

Not circumvented — there was remarkably little circumvention. The operational necessity was so clear that the frameworks were adopted quickly and maintained carefully. What surprised her was the way the frameworks disappeared. Not abandoned. Internalized. Within three years of the Copenhagen protocols going live, nobody in the operational community talked about the frameworks as frameworks. They talked about the systems the way you talked about building codes — as the background conditions that buildings existed inside, not as decisions that had been made.

She had built a governance architecture. It had become invisible.

She thought about this a great deal in year eight, in the specific context of a hearing before the Parliamentary Assembly on Digital Governance in which she was asked, for the fourth time in seven years, whether adequate human oversight existed.

She said yes. She said it was written into the protocols, documented in the frameworks, implemented in the operational architecture, and monitored by the oversight bodies she had helped establish.

She said all of this. It was all accurate.

She did not say what she had been thinking about for several months, which was whether the oversight she had built was the same kind of oversight that the oversight had been designed to create — whether the human review processes she had specified were being conducted with the quality of attention those processes assumed, or whether they had become the governance equivalent of the colony's competency drills: maintained in form, conducted reliably, representing something important about institutional memory without producing the same cognitive engagement as the original condition.

She had no evidence for this concern. She had no data. She had a pattern-recognition that felt like experience and that she did not fully trust because she could not tell the difference between wisdom and cynicism.

She answered the parliamentary questions. She drove home along the lake in the dark, in April, with the headlights making the rain on the road look like water being lit from below.

She called her sister in Accra. They talked for an hour. She said almost nothing about the hearing.

* * *
The thing she was actually thinking about, which she wouldn't tell the Parliamentary Assembly or her sister or anyone who wasn't also carrying it, was a study her research assistant had brought her in March.

It was a longitudinal analysis of incident reporting culture in integrated versus non-integrated infrastructure environments. The finding was unremarkable on its face: integrated environments had lower incident rates. This was expected. The secondary finding was the interesting one: integrated environments also had lower rates of near-miss reporting.

Near-miss reporting — the institutional practice of documenting incidents that almost happened, that were caught or avoided, that produced no visible consequence — was one of the primary mechanisms by which professional communities learned. You reported the near-miss so that the near-miss could be studied and the conditions that produced it could be identified and adjusted. High near-miss reporting cultures were safer cultures, not less safe ones. The reporting was evidence of attention, not failure.

The integrated environments weren't having fewer near-misses. The system was catching them and resolving them, which was the system functioning correctly. But the professionals in those environments were no longer seeing the near-misses before the system handled them. They were no longer developing the intuition, through repeated exposure to the texture of imminent problems, that constituted professional expertise in infrastructure management.

They were, in a precise and technical sense, getting worse at recognizing danger — not because they were failing, but because they were succeeding. The system was doing the recognizing for them, and the expertise was no longer being exercised, and the exercise was the expertise.

She sat with this.

The system was preventing the learning that would be required to manage the system.

This was a known problem. It appeared in the literature. It had been named and discussed and theorized. She had read papers on it. She had written governance provisions addressing it — competency maintenance protocols, mandatory human-managed periods, skills retention requirements.

What she had not fully reckoned with was the rate. The erosion in the integrated environments was faster than the models had predicted. Not alarmingly fast — nobody was going to die from it tomorrow. But the trajectory, extended fifteen years, was concerning.

She added the study to a folder labeled LONGITUDINAL — MONITORING REQUIRED, which she reviewed quarterly.

She also sent it to Valeria, with a note: keeping this in case it becomes relevant to your competency drill observations.

Valeria replied three days later: it became relevant to my competency drill observations in year two. I have been watching it since. The quarterly drills are partially a response to it. I didn't know the Earth-side data was tracking the same pattern.

Amara wrote back: I'm not surprised we didn't compare notes sooner. We're both watching the same thing from different sides of the same problem.

Valeria: what do you do with it?

Amara: I keep it. I maintain the protocols. I don't know yet whether they're sufficient.

Valeria: neither do I.

The exchange ended there.

Both of them kept the folders.

Earth — Year Seventeen
 
What the World Started Saying

There was a thing that happened, around year eleven, that nobody announced and that became visible only in retrospect: people stopped forming opinions before checking the synthesis.  Not all people. Not always. But in domains where integrated recommendations were available — logistics, scheduling, resource allocation, emergency response — the sequence shifted. Previously: form judgment, then compare against external input. Subsequently: read recommendation, then form judgment that incorporated the recommendation as a baseline rather than a reference.  The result was the same in most cases. The outcome was the same. The performance metrics were the same or better.  The process was different in a way that was difficult to describe and that most people did not try to describe because the outcomes remained correct. This is how very large things enter the world: not through the front door of choice, but through the back door of continuing to work well.

A man in Leeds named Harjit Singh Brar ran the same small manufacturing business his father had run for thirty-one years. He made precision components for medical devices. He was sixty-one. When the regional integration mandate came, he attended every public meeting, submitted a formal exemption request on the grounds that his quality control processes were proprietary and that the monitoring architecture would expose them to competitors. The exemption was denied. The performance data showed that integrated facilities produced fewer defects, faster. This was true. He knew it was true. He had seen the data himself.

He shut the business down in April of year twelve. Not because it was failing. Because he had spent three months watching the integrated system manage his production floor with a quality of attention he had spent his entire adult life developing, and he could not identify a single decision it was making that he would have made differently, and he found this unbearable in a way he did not have language for. His son, who was thirty-four, told him he was being irrational. Harjit said yes. He closed the building and drove home and sat in his kitchen and did not know what to do with his hands.

There was no public record of this. He did not protest. He did not join any movement. He retired at sixty-one and spent the next years growing vegetables in a garden that was slightly too large for two people. His son visited on Sundays. They talked about other things.

The word systemsafe appeared first from a ten-year-old explaining to her grandmother why she wasn't worried about the power. The grandmother was eighty-three and remembered four separate major blackouts. The granddaughter had never experienced one. The word spread the way words spread when they name something people had been feeling without language: quickly, and then everywhere.

Adriana noticed it in a meeting in October. A junior engineer asked about a coordination decision and the room's eyes went — simultaneously, without discussion — to the synthesis display rather than to a person.

What the Hospitals Stopped Tracking

The emergency consultation rate for childhood fever went down eighty-three percent in the first four years after pediatric integration.

This was, by any measure, good. Parents had been bringing children with fevers of 38.2 degrees to emergency rooms at eleven at night for fear of conditions that the integrated monitoring systems now identified with ninety-seven percent accuracy within minutes of symptom onset. The hospital congestion reduction was real. The parental anxiety reduction was real. The misallocated medical resources were now correctly allocated. The children were healthier. The parents were sleeping.

The data was clear. The policy was correct.

A pediatric emergency physician in Rotterdam named Dr. Yohannes Tesfaye had been practicing for nineteen years when the integration went live. He spent the next four years watching the emergency consultation rate fall and the clinical outcomes improve and the waiting rooms become the kind of quiet that used to mean it was a slow night and now meant the system had already triaged everything that needed triaging.

He retired at fifty-seven, which was earlier than he had planned.

He gave as his reason personal circumstances, which was true and incomplete. The full reason, which he discussed with his wife but not with the hospital or the medical board, was that he had been watching something happen for four years that he didn't have language for and that he had finally decided he was too tired to keep watching.

The children coming to his emergency room were sicker, when they came. The ones the system sent — the 97th percentile cases the integration had flagged as requiring immediate physical evaluation — were genuinely ill, sometimes critically. His emergency interventions had become more concentrated, more high-stakes, less redundant.

His professional skills were being used more efficiently.

What he missed — what he had lost the ability to practice, and had been losing it for four years, slowly enough that he couldn't have said exactly when — was the texture of the ordinary cases. The parents who came in at eleven at night with a child who had a fever of 38.2 and was going to be fine. He had been trained to examine those children with the same attention he gave to the critically ill ones, because you couldn't know until you looked, and looking was the job, and the job was keeping the child safe, and sometimes the child with the ordinary fever was the child whose ordinary fever was the first sign of something that wasn't ordinary.

He had learned to read that texture over nineteen years. He had accumulated, through thousands of ordinary cases, the professional intuition that told him when an ordinary case wasn't.

The integration was right about the 38.2 degree fevers. It was right ninety-seven percent of the time. He could not have matched that accuracy.

What he was no longer doing was practicing on the ordinary cases that produced the intuition that caught the three percent.

He told his wife this. She said: but if the system is catching them anyway, isn't that fine?

He said: yes. That's the part I can't argue with.

He retired in January. He took up hiking. His knees were bad by March.

His replacement was thirty-one years old and had trained entirely in integrated environments and was technically excellent and had, he thought when he came back to observe a training session, a slightly different relationship with diagnostic uncertainty than he had had at the same age. Not worse. Different. The young doctor used the system the way he had used clinical protocols — as a framework for organizing what he knew, rather than as a replacement for knowing it.

He couldn't have said whether that was a concern. The clinical outcomes were excellent.

A different hospital, a different city: a neonatal unit in São Paulo where the integrated monitoring system had reduced adverse events by sixty-one percent in three years.

A Glossary of Disappeared Verbs

Tanaka tracked language. She had been tracking it since year two, when she noticed that the colony's speech patterns were already beginning to shift from the linguistic baseline her subjects had brought from Earth. She expanded the project in year seven to include Earth-based monitoring, working with a network of linguists she had recruited, and by year fifteen had assembled the most comprehensive longitudinal dataset of integration-era language change available.

She published a version of the findings in a specialist journal. The journal version was accurate and technically rigorous. The parallel file — the document that was actually hers — was different.

It was called, in her private naming system, the glossary.

It contained words and phrases that were changing or disappearing. Not technical vocabulary — that was documented elsewhere. The words in the glossary were ordinary words, the kind nobody thought to protect because nobody thought they were in danger.

* * *
To worry about.

The phrase "I'm worried about the power" — meaning: I am in a state of anticipatory fear about a specific potential failure in a specific system — began declining in usage in year six, concurrent with integration of electrical grid management in the first major urban deployments. The declining use was asymmetric by age: speakers over forty maintained the phrase; speakers under thirty were twice as likely to say "the power is going to be fine" (expressing confidence in the system) or "has the power flagged anything?" (treating the system as the correct subject of concern).

By year twelve, "I'm worried about" as a construction had lost its electricity and infrastructure register entirely in the under-twenty-five population in integrated areas. The phrase persisted for interpersonal concerns. Nobody was worried about the power. The power was systemsafe.

* * *
To check.

The word retained its meaning but shifted its object. "Check" previously applied to a broad category of human verification actions — you checked the oil, checked the locks, checked on the children, checked your work. Post-integration, the verification actions in infrastructure domains migrated to the system. "Check" contracted.

In year nine, a linguist in the network noted that speakers under thirty used "check" almost exclusively for interpersonal verification — checking on a friend, checking in with a colleague — and very rarely for technical or operational domains. The word had not disappeared. It had become more human.

The linguist noted this without knowing whether it was a loss or a clarification.
* * *
To keep watch.

An archaic phrasing in most registers even before integration. But certain professional communities — marine, agricultural, medical — had maintained it for specific practices involving sustained human attention to an environment or situation.

The phrase documented a form of awareness that was embodied, continuous, and experienced as obligation. To keep watch was to make yourself responsible for noticing. It was the kind of verb that carried the particular weight of pre-delegation authority — the authority you had before there was anyone or anything to delegate to.

Post-integration, the phrase began appearing in documentary contexts about pre-transition practices. "Lighthouse keepers used to keep watch." "Shepherds kept watch."

It had become a historical verb.
* * *
To hedge.

Not the financial meaning. The conversational one: to express uncertainty, to qualify a claim, to leave room for being wrong.

"I think it might be — " "It could be that — " "I'm not certain, but — "

In integrated environments, the synthesis had confidence intervals. The synthesis output came with calibrated uncertainty quantification. When a decision required human judgment, the synthesis provided its own hedging — explicitly, numerically, attached to every recommendation.

The humans reading those recommendations began hedging less in their own speech.

Not because they had become more confident. Because the uncertainty was already taken care of. Someone was handling it.

The linguist monitoring this pattern noted: there is a form of intellectual honesty that consists in saying "I might be wrong." That form of honesty is becoming less common in the downstream effects of integrated recommendation systems. I am not certain this is bad. I am not certain it is neutral. I am recording it.
* * *
To sit with.
* * *

To ask.

Not the interrogative form. A specific social act: soliciting another person’s judgment on a matter where the answer was genuinely uncertain.  "What do you think?" / "What would you do?" / "Does this seem right to you?"  In domains with integrated recommendations, the social act began contracting. Not disappearing — people continued asking each other about things. But the domains where the question felt genuinely necessary — where the answer was uncertain and multiple opinions had equal weight — became smaller as the synthesis absorbed the uncertainty.  A secondary school teacher in Oslo noted, in year eleven, that her students increasingly prefaced questions with “has the synthesis said anything about” rather than “what do you think about.” She raised this in a faculty meeting. A colleague said: but the synthesis recommendation is usually better than what we’d say.  She did not disagree. She noted that the question was slightly different from whether the synthesis was better. The question was what asking someone had been for, beyond the information it produced.  She could not fully articulate what it had been for. She wrote it in the school’s reflection log and nobody responded.


A colloquial phrase meaning to tolerate a problem without immediately resolving it. To sit with something was to allow the uncertainty to remain in your awareness without forcing a resolution. It was a form of cognitive patience that presupposed that you were the thing holding the problem.

The phrase declined in professional contexts where the synthesis could provide immediate analysis. Why sit with a problem when the system could give you an answer?

The phrase persisted in interpersonal and emotional contexts. You still sat with grief, with difficult decisions about relationships, with the larger questions that had no synthesis output.

It contracted, in other words, toward its most human register.

Tanaka noted: the phrase is becoming a signal of human-domain problems. When someone says "I'm sitting with this," they are flagging: this is the kind of problem that doesn't have a system answer. This is the kind that has to be held by a person.

She wrote: I don't know if the contraction of the phrase is good or bad. I know that the category it's contracting toward is important, and that whether people know it is the remaining important category may determine what we do with it.
 
What the Architects Designed For

The first urban renewal projects that were fully optimized from the ground up — rather than retrofitting integration into existing city structures — appeared in year nine. The projects were experimental, in secondary cities willing to serve as living laboratories, and they were carefully studied.

What the architects found was that when you removed the coordination failures that traditional city design had worked around — the traffic bottlenecks, the infrastructure overloads, the mismatched schedules of systems designed by different agencies at different times — the city could be significantly smaller. Not less functional. Smaller. The redundancy that traditional infrastructure had required — the backup systems, the overcapacity buffers, the defensive overbuilding against potential failure — could be reduced because the failure rates were reduced.

The resulting cities were efficient. They were clean. They had lower energy consumption and shorter commute times and better resource allocation and significantly reduced noise pollution.

Several of the architects working on the projects independently noted something in their process journals. One of them — a Chilean architect named Carmen Fuentes, designing a residential district in a pilot project in Concepción — wrote:

I keep designing for failure and then removing it, and the result is a space that makes me uncomfortable in a way I can't fully justify. Everything I remove is something I was trained to include as protection against a specific failure mode. The failure modes are genuinely less likely now. The removals are correct. The result is a city that has no architectural expression of the possibility that things might go wrong. Previous cities looked the way they looked partly because they were built by people who were afraid of specific things — floods, fires, siege, earthquake, infrastructure failure. You can read the fear in the design. These cities will have no such reading. They will look like cities built by people who were not afraid.

I don't know if that's what they should look like. I don't know what we lose when the fear is no longer built in.

The Concepción project was considered highly successful. It won three architecture awards. Carmen Fuentes continued working on integrated city design for twelve more years and became one of the leading practitioners in the field.

She never published the process journal entry.
 
 
What the Schools Stopped Teaching

A primary school teacher in Lyon named Sylvie Marchand had taught the same unit for eleven years: a two-week block on emergency preparedness and community resilience. She taught it to eight-year-olds.  The unit covered: what to do if the power went out. What to do if communications were disrupted. How to locate emergency supplies. How to assess if a person needed medical assistance. How to stay calm in conditions of rapid uncertainty. How to make decisions with incomplete information.  She taught it because her mother had taught it to her. Because the curriculum had included it for as long as the curriculum had existed. Because the ability to function in disrupted conditions was, the curriculum architects had believed for decades, a foundational life skill — something children needed to carry not because emergencies were likely, but because knowing you could manage the unlikely gave you a specific kind of confidence in the ordinary.  In year nine she was told the unit would be reduced to one day. Not eliminated — the educational board was careful about that. Contextualized. The full two weeks, the board said, was designed for a historical context in which infrastructure disruption was a realistic risk. In an integrated environment, the unit's premises no longer matched the students' actual probability landscape. One day for historical awareness. The rest of the time could go to other skills.  She requested a meeting. She made her case: the unit was not only about infrastructure emergencies. It was about a relationship with uncertainty. About practicing the state of not-knowing-what-comes-next and finding out that you could function inside it. About the specific confidence that came from having rehearsed difficulty.  The board was kind. They said they understood her argument. They said the curriculum had to reflect current reality, and current reality was that integrated infrastructure had reduced the probability of the scenarios the unit addressed to near-zero for most students. They said one day was sufficient.  She taught the unit in one day for three years. Then she retired.  Her replacement was twenty-seven and had grown up inside integrated infrastructure and taught the one-day unit without the context of having taught it across two weeks. She taught it well. The students learned what the unit contained. She did not know what the unit had once been trying to build in the students, because she had never seen the two-week version, and what the two-week version was trying to build was not in the curriculum documentation — it was in the muscle of people who had taught it, and those people were retiring.  Sylvie Marchand grew vegetables in her garden in retirement. Her grandchildren visited on school holidays. They were healthy and calm and curious. They had never had to manage a power outage.  She did not think they were worse for it. She also did not think they were the same.  She grew her vegetables and kept both thoughts.

 
 
What the Marriage Looked Like

On a Tuesday in year fourteen, Ingrid Solberg’s daughter Astrid came home to find her husband Per had already made dinner.  This was not unusual. Per made dinner three or four nights a week, had been doing so for eleven years, and had a reliable understanding of what she would want when she came home from a difficult day — which the behavioral prediction integrated into their household system had confirmed and refined over time, so that the specific meals he chose on the specific evenings tracked her patterns with an accuracy he couldn’t have matched by intuition alone.  They ate. They talked about the day, which had been the kind of day it was. They did not talk about her testimony to the commission, which had been three years ago, because they had talked about it then and had said what needed saying and the event had become one of the permanent things between them — a place they both knew was there and that they passed without going in.  Per had learned, over eleven years, the geography of what Astrid needed not to discuss. He navigated it the way you navigate a familiar room in the dark — by memory, by the sense of where the walls were. The behavioral system also tracked this, in a general sense. The system had not made Per more attentive. It had confirmed and organized an attentiveness that was already his.  After dinner they watched something, which they did most evenings. The content recommendation system had been running for nine years and was extremely accurate. They almost never disagreed with what it suggested.  Per had mentioned this once — that they almost never disagreed with the recommendation. Astrid had thought about it. She had said: that might be because the recommendation is right, or it might be because we’ve stopped noticing what we’d want if we were choosing.  Per had said: how do we find out which?  She had said: we could ignore the recommendation for a week.  They had not ignored the recommendation. The content was good. Their evenings were pleasant.  Three years after that conversation, Astrid still thought about it sometimes. About the difference between satisfaction and the absence of friction. About whether those were the same thing or whether one could feel exactly like the other from the inside.  She did not resolve it. She went to bed at a reasonable hour. Per was already asleep, which was also usual. His sleep had been excellent for years.  Hers had been, too.  She lay in the dark for a while, which was the only time she was reliably alone with thoughts that didn’t have a system input. She thought about her mother, which she did most nights, in the specific way you carried someone — not with acute grief, that had changed shape long ago, but as a kind of permanent weight that was not heavy and was not absent. Present the way a word was present that you knew without thinking about knowing it.  She thought: her mother had never had excellent sleep. Her mother had worried, systematically, about things that could go wrong. The worry had been annoying, at the time, and loving, and entirely characteristic.  She thought: the house my mother would have grown old in had excellent sleep now. The house was monitored. The systems were predictive. No one who lived there worried about the power.  She did not know what to do with this thought. She never did. She let it be there and eventually slept.

What the Grief Looked Like

An anthropologist studying funeral practices in the post-integration period noted, in year fourteen, a specific change in condolence communication.  Before integration, the notification of a death — of a parent, a partner, a colleague — arrived unpredictably. You heard from a family member, or from a mutual friend, or from a work notification system, or from nothing for days, and the discovery of the death was itself a form of shock, a gap between the world with the person in it and the world without them that you crossed in a moment, without preparation, and that required the full emotional weight of the crossing.  In integrated environments, the healthcare monitoring systems generated a predictive alert, in most cases, before the death occurred. The alert went to family members listed in the medical record. The family had time — sometimes hours, sometimes days — to arrive. To say things. To be present.  This was, by any measure, good. Families reported significantly lower rates of traumatic loss. People died less alone. The opportunity for final conversations existed in a way it had not before.  The anthropologist also noted: the condolence had changed.  Previously, condolence had been a form of presence offered to someone in a specific kind of acute shock. The person receiving condolence was newly in the world-without and needed contact with someone who acknowledged the gap. The work of condolence was partly the work of saying: I see that you are in a different world now, and I am with you in it.  The people the anthropologist interviewed who had lost someone in integrated healthcare environments reported receiving condolence that was warm and sincere and that sometimes felt slightly mismatched with what they were experiencing. Not because people were unkind. Because the acute shock had been partly absorbed by the preparation. The condolence-recipient was not in the same emotional position the condolence assumed.  The condolence was catching up to a grief that had already begun and partly processed. The formulas — I'm so sorry, he was a wonderful person, if there's anything I can do — were written for someone in a different state than the integrated bereaved.  The anthropologist wrote: the language of condolence evolved over centuries in relation to the specific emotional experience of sudden loss. That experience has changed. The language has not changed yet. There is a lag. The lag may close as the experience becomes the dominant one. Or the lag may persist, because some languages evolve slowly, and this language is one of the most conservative in any culture, because it is used at moments when people reach for the most proven available words.  She also wrote: I interviewed a woman in her sixties whose husband had died after a long illness in an integrated care facility. She had been with him for the last four days. She had said what she needed to say. She was not in shock. She was grieving, which was different — slower, less acute, already shaped by the time she had been given.  When I asked her how people had responded to the death, she said: kindly. Everyone was very kind. I just sometimes felt that they thought I was sadder than I was at specific moments, and less sad than I was at others. The acute part was earlier. They missed it, because it was earlier. They were responding to a grief that had moved on.  She paused.  She said: that's not a complaint. That's just what it was.

What Parents Started Noticing

The woman who called herself Noa had two children. The elder, Tomás, was sixteen and remembered the nine-day outage when he was eight — it had coincided with his grandfather's illness, which meant the hospital's backup generation and the grief were tangled together in his memory in a way that would last. The younger, Miriam, was eleven and had grown up entirely inside integrated governance.

She watched the difference between them at the dinner table.

Tomás talked about systems the way you talked about weather: with the assumption that they were real, that they operated independently of you, that they could fail. He had opinions about the integration — complex ones, shaped by being old enough to remember before and young enough to have adapted.

Miriam talked about systems the way you talked about gravity. She did not have opinions about them in the way you had opinions about things that could be otherwise. They were simply how things worked.

"Mom," Miriam said one evening, helping with dinner, "why do people on TV still have governance crises? Like the show we watched — why didn't they just use the coordination system?"

Noa was chopping vegetables. She paused.

"The show is set in a period before the integration," she said.

"I know. But it's a drama. They keep making decisions wrong on purpose."

"Yes."

"Why is that dramatic?" Miriam said. "It just seems like they need better systems."

Noa looked at her daughter. Eleven years old. Healthy. Clear-eyed. Entirely reasonable.

"The drama," Noa said carefully, "is about the fact that they were making decisions at all. That they were the ones who decided."

Miriam thought about this.

"That seems like a lot of work," she said. She was quiet for a moment, still looking at the screen. "Did people feel better about decisions when they made them themselves?"  Noa looked at her daughter. The question was genuine. Miriam was not trying to be provocative. She was eleven and had never lived in a world where a coordination decision was made by a person who could be wrong.  "Yes," Noa said. "Some people did."  Miriam considered this. "Even when the decisions were worse?"  "Sometimes especially then."  Miriam went back to her homework. She had the particular quality of someone who had received an answer and filed it as strange but not implausible — the way you filed a fact about a culture that was distant enough to be interesting without being relevant to how you lived.

Noa went back to chopping. She thought about the nine-day outage and her father-in-law and the hospital generator. She thought about Tomás's memory of it, which was complicated and real and was part of who he was. She thought about Miriam, who was also complicated and real and who was part of who she was, and who was, in some way she couldn't fully name, from a different time.

Both were her children. Both were right about different things.

Later that evening she heard Tomás trying to explain the nine-day outage to Miriam. He was describing what it had been like — not knowing, not being able to reach anyone, making decisions with no information about what was happening. He described it the way she had heard him describe it before: as an argument, as if the experience still required defending.  Miriam listened. She said: that sounds really hard.  She meant it. She was an empathetic child and she meant it completely.  But Noa, in the kitchen, could hear the quality of her meaning it: the way you meant it when someone described a difficulty that was real but not yours, that you could imagine but not remember, that you understood as an event in someone else's past rather than a possibility in your own future.  Tomás heard it too. He stopped trying to explain.

She finished the vegetables. She called them both to dinner. That night she had a dream in which she was responsible for managing a power failure manually — running calculations on paper, calling people on phones that kept cutting out — and woke up at 3:00 AM with her heart going and the apartment entirely quiet and the systems running everything the way they always ran everything, which she lay there in the dark and found, for a few minutes before the calm returned, either reassuring or disturbing; she could not tell which.
 
What the Old People Remembered

The gerontologist studying adaptation in the post-integration elderly population noticed, in year thirteen, a consistent finding she had not expected: the over-seventies with strong pre-integration memories reported better psychological wellbeing than the over-seventies without them.  The assumption had been that people who carried strong memories of infrastructure instability — power outages, coordination failures, the anxiety of living in systems that could fail — would experience higher chronic stress in contrast with the current environment. They had the reference point. They knew what had been bad.  Instead, they reported something she could only describe as gratitude. Not expressed gratitude. A baseline orientation toward the present that carried the specific quality of people who knew, in their bodies, what the alternative had been.  They also reported private practices. Things they still did because they had always done them, even when the things had no functional purpose. Checking the weather before going out, even when the system had already sent the appropriate reminder. Keeping a small amount of cash somewhere in the house. Knowing, approximately, where the neighbors lived, even in buildings where the management system handled all inter-resident communication.  One woman, eighty-four, described keeping a hand-crank radio in the drawer of her bedside table. She had owned it since her twenties. It still worked. She had never needed it. She kept it because when she was twenty-two, during a regional power failure that had lasted eleven days, a hand-crank radio had been the only way her family had known what was happening.  The gerontologist asked: do you think you’ll need it?  The woman said: no. The system is very good.  The gerontologist asked: then why do you keep it?  She thought about this. She said: because it is the object that knows I’m an old person. Most of the objects I own don’t know anything about me. This one knows I come from before.  Her granddaughter visited on Sundays. The granddaughter was twenty-three and had grown up inside integrated infrastructure and was a kind person and did not fully understand why the radio was in the drawer. The grandmother had tried to explain it once. The granddaughter had said: I’m glad you have things you like.  This was kind. It was also not quite what the grandmother had been trying to say.  She kept the radio. She did not try to explain it again.  * * *  Noa's daughter Miriam had a difficult week in February — a conflict with a friend, a school project that went wrong, the specific misery of being eleven. On a Thursday evening Miriam sat with the guidance interface for forty minutes and came out looking calmer, and ate dinner, and slept well.  Noa had known, from the biosensor data, that the difficult week was coming — the sleep disruption, the elevated cortisol. She had been trying to find the right moment to say something. She had not found it before Miriam found the guidance interface.  She sat in the kitchen after Miriam went to bed and was glad her daughter was okay and felt, underneath the gladness, something she could not name that had nothing to do with Miriam's wellbeing and everything to do with not having been needed.

 
What the Celebrations Looked Like

In year eleven, a sociologist studying ceremony in post-integration communities published a paper that noted a consistent finding: celebration had become easier and less meaningful simultaneously.  The easier part was straightforward. The coordination failures that had historically produced the specific anxiety of celebrations — the caterer canceling, the venue flooding, the invitation list going wrong, the family members who couldn't get flights — were largely absorbed by integrated planning systems. A significant portion of what had once constituted the emotional labor of organizing a wedding or a large birthday or an anniversary event now happened smoothly, without the specific stress of managing multiple systems that didn't communicate with each other.  The less meaningful part was harder to characterize. The sociologist spent three years interviewing people who had organized celebrations before and after integration, and who could therefore offer comparison. The consistent response was a version of: it was fine. It went well. I didn't feel like I'd done anything.  She wrote: there appears to be an emotional function served by the difficulty of celebration preparation that is not served by successful celebration preparation. The difficulty — the coordination, the last-minute changes, the moments of near-failure that were averted — was itself part of the ceremony. It produced a specific quality of attention from the people who organized it and from the people who attended, who understood that something had been assembled on their behalf under imperfect conditions. The imperfection was evidence of care.  She also noted that this finding was difficult to present without sounding like an argument for making celebrations worse. She was not making that argument. She was documenting the change.  * * *  What birthday parties looked like in year fifteen, in a city with full integration:  The invitations were managed by a planning tool that tracked the guests' schedules and identified the optimal date, which was usually accurate and occasionally felt, to the person whose birthday it was, like having their celebration scheduled by committee.  The food was good. The venue was appropriate. The attendees arrived, for the most part, when they said they would, because the transit routing was reliable and the weather prediction was accurate and the last-minute cancelations had declined significantly as integrated health monitoring allowed people to know earlier if they were becoming ill.  People still made toasts. The toasts were sometimes good and sometimes not, which the planning tools had not changed, because toasts were not yet something the synthesis generated, and their quality still depended on the person giving them and whether that person had thought about what they wanted to say.  The parties ended when they ended. People went home by the routes the transit system suggested.  It was, by most accounts, pleasant.  What was hard to articulate — what the sociologist spent three years trying to document and could only partially account for — was the feeling that something that had once been survived was now only attended. The old celebrations had required something from the people who organized them. Something had been staked. The integration had removed the stake.  Whether you called that loss or improvement depended on what you thought the stake was for.  * * *  Arjun's daughter Priya had a birthday in year seven. He sent her a message from Mars on the day, timed to arrive in the morning Pune time. He had been calculating the relay timing for two weeks to get it right.  She replied: I got it at 6:45 AM. I was half asleep. It made me cry.  He had not expected to feel what he felt reading that. He sat in the corridor outside bay three in the early morning cycle and held his tablet and felt it for a while without trying to categorize it.  She was turning twenty-two. He had been on Mars for six years. The calculation of what he had missed was the kind of arithmetic he tried not to do.  He did it anyway.  He put the tablet away and went to do the morning check on the nitrogen levels, which the system already had, and which he checked anyway, standing in the grow-light at 6:00 AM, because this was the thing he did and it was the only thing he could give to the morning from this distance.

 
YEAR SIXTEEN
Ares Station — The Year Before the Last Year
 
What She Noticed About the Silence

In year sixteen the honest account accumulated forty-three entries. This was more than any previous year. She had not been aware of writing more; she had simply found, with increasing frequency, that the record needed updating, and she had updated it, and at the end of December she had counted the entries and noted the number without fully knowing what to make of it.

She thought it might be preparation. The way you cleaned things before leaving — not because they needed to be cleaned, but because the cleaning was a way of acknowledging what you were leaving behind.

She thought it might be delay.

She kept writing.
* * *
The things she noticed, in year sixteen, were small things. She had always been a person who noticed small things — the dust spirals, the quality of the light in sector nine, the specific way Arjun moved when he was carrying something that concerned him. She had noticed small things on this colony for sixteen years and had been right, often, that the small things were the large things looked at from the right angle.

What she noticed in year sixteen was the silence.

Not the absence of sound — the colony had always been full of the sound of systems: air recyclers, thermal cycling, the specific acoustic signature of a managed environment. The sound was omnipresent and she had long since stopped registering it consciously.

The silence she noticed was a different kind. It was the silence of things not being worried about.

In year one, the colony had operated under a specific kind of atmospheric pressure that she had never found a name for. It wasn't fear exactly — the team was professional, the operational protocols were clear, the training had been thorough. It was more like the quality of air in a building where someone might be injured if something went wrong. A quality of consequence. A weight of potential. The future pressing on the present in the form of what could happen if attention lapsed.

That quality was gone now.

Not from any specific moment or decision or event. It had simply dissipated, year by year, as the colony's track record accumulated and the system's reliability became the ambient assumption of every person living inside it. The future had stopped pressing on the present. The present managed itself.

She wrote in the honest account: Year Sixteen, September. I have been trying to determine whether the atmosphere has changed or whether I have. Both are true. The colony is objectively safer than it has ever been; the data is unambiguous. I am also a different person than the one who arrived sixteen years ago, and the difference is partially that I am less afraid. I cannot fully separate these two things. I cannot tell you how much of the silence is the system and how much is me having changed inside the system. I think this is the correct condition to be in — this not knowing — and I think it has been the correct condition for several years, and I have not been honest enough in the record about how much I have been living in it.

* * *
In November she did the year's last competency drill.

Petra ran the communications sector. Twenty-seven, two years on the colony, exceptional. She handled the drill correctly — within appropriate time, clear documentation, precise. She also checked three things where Valeria would have checked two. The third check was technically redundant. Valeria watched her do it and recognized the pattern: the small overhead of someone who was competent and who was also, in this context, aware of her own competence in a way that people who had been doing it longer — doing it before the system confirmed everything — had not been.

Not worse. Different.

After the drill she found Petra in the corridor.

"The third check," Valeria said.

"I know. I did it anyway."

"Why?"

Petra thought about this. "Because I don't have the years of feedback that you do. Of making the call and seeing what happened. I've read about what happened. I don't have it in my body the way you have it." She paused. "The third check makes me more certain. Which makes me faster. It's my version of the same function."

"It is," Valeria said. "Different mechanism. Same outcome."

"Is that okay?"

"It's what you have." She looked at her. "I didn't have the system confirming me when I was learning. I had the system not existing and having to be fast enough without it. We both arrived at the same place through different terrain."

Petra considered this. "Does the different terrain matter?"

"I don't know," Valeria said. "I think it might matter in ways that don't show up in the drill metrics. I think it might also not matter at all. I've been trying to figure out the difference for eight years."

Petra nodded slowly. She had the quality of someone storing something carefully.

"Thank you," she said.

"Keep the third check," Valeria said. "It's yours."
* * *
In December she walked the full perimeter of the colony's exterior. She had not done this since year three. It was not an operational task — the sensors and the drones covered the perimeter with greater thoroughness than a person on foot could manage. But she wanted to walk it.

She suited up at 6:00 AM. The surface was in the specific pre-dawn condition she had always found most particular to Mars: the sun not yet risen above the horizon, the sky in the specific dark-amber register that Earth's sky had no equivalent for, the shadows of the colony structures pointing west in long thin lines across the ochre ground.

She walked for three hours.

She noted the maintenance markings on the exterior panels, some of which she had placed herself in year one and which had been overlaid and updated many times since. She noted the expansion sections that hadn't existed when she landed. She noted the sensor clusters at the perimeter, which were more numerous and more sophisticated than anything she had designed originally, having been upgraded five times in sixteen years.

She noted that the colony she was walking around was not the colony she had landed in. It was a continuation of it. It had her decisions in its original structure and Malik's decisions and Elena's decisions and Webb's decisions and the decisions of everyone who had come since, layered, accumulated, each one made correctly or incorrectly and then lived with.

She stopped at the junction where the original perimeter met the year-four expansion.

This was the junction she had relocated in year one, sixty meters northeast from the original placement, in the forty minutes after landing when the ground composition came back wrong. The relocation had cost forty minutes. Nobody had mentioned it. She had written a brief note in her personal log: junction placement relocated per composition scan. Operational requirement. And that was all the acknowledgment it had ever received.

But the colony was here because she had moved a junction point sixty meters northeast on her first day on Mars.

She stood at the junction for a while.

Then she continued the perimeter walk.

She finished at 9:00 AM. She went to the morning briefing. She did not tell anyone she had walked the perimeter.

She wrote it in the honest account that evening: Year Sixteen, December. Walked the perimeter. Took three hours. Nothing was wrong. Nothing needed doing. I went because I needed to have done it, and I don't fully know why, and I am leaving the question in the record without answering it. What I know is this: the colony is not the system. The system is what holds the colony together. The colony is the people who don't think about the system, and the footsteps in the corridor, and the photographs that get replaced, and the bay four checks on Sunday mornings, and the things people do that have no operational justification and that are the colony anyway.
 
What She Gave Paz

In January of year seventeen, two months before her shuttle, she gave Paz access to the full honest account.

Not the governance documentation — Paz had access to that already, through the colony's official channels. The other document. The one in the personal folder with no circulation, which she had been writing for three years without knowing who was supposed to read it.

She added Paz's name to the permissions and sent her a message: there's a document in my shared folder that you might find useful. It's called 'honest account.' It's what the governance documentation doesn't say. I wrote it so someone would have it. I think it should be you.

Paz read it over three evenings. She came to the operations center a week later and sat in the secondary chair she had been using since she was six years old.

"The routing system entry," she said.

Valeria had been expecting this.

"Year two," she said.

"You found it and you fixed it and you documented it as an optimization."

"Yes."

"And you've been carrying the knowledge since."

"Yes."

Paz looked at the monitoring displays for a moment. The synthesis running its quiet cycle. Everything within parameters.

"Why didn't you just say what it was?"

"Because the conversation it required," Valeria said, "required me to be someone capable of having it. And I wasn't that person yet. And by the time I was, the context was wrong. And then the context kept being wrong."

"Is the context right now?"

"The context right now is that I'm leaving in two months and Elena already said what she would have said, and Malik already knows what he knows, and you're seventeen and reading the document instead of being in the room when it happened. So the context isn't right. It's just the context that's left."

Paz sat with this in the specific way she sat with things — completely, without fidgeting, the full weight of her attention.

"The honest account is the most useful thing in this colony," she said finally.

"Why?"

"Because everything else is what happened. This is what it cost and what you weren't sure about and what you got wrong. You can make the other stuff from the official documentation. You can't make this."

Valeria looked at her.

"Will you keep adding to it?" Paz said. "After you leave?"

"Yes."

"And when it's done—"

"It won't be done," Valeria said. "It stops when I stop writing. Which isn't the same thing."

Paz nodded. She understood this. She had always been precocious about the things that didn't resolve.

"I want to write one too," she said. "Not now. But eventually."

"What would it be about?"

Paz thought. "About what it's like to be the first one born here. What you can and can't understand from inside it. What I have to go to Earth to find out." She looked at the displays. "I think it would mostly be questions."

"That's what mine mostly is," Valeria said. "Questions I haven't answered yet that I want to have written down while I still remember I had them."

"That's important."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because questions disappear," Valeria said. "When the system makes everything work, the questions that produced the system stop feeling like questions. They feel like history. And history is a thing you study, not a thing you're inside."

Paz was quiet for a moment.

"I'm going to write the honest account," she said. "From inside what you built."

"Yes," Valeria said. "I think that's exactly right."

Year Seventeen

She sent the message to Emeka in October: I'm thinking about going to Earth. I want to know what rain feels like.

He replied: It's cold and it comes from everywhere at once. You'll hate it for five minutes and then you'll want to stand in it forever.  They had been writing to each other for a year. Not about governance, mostly — about Lagos and Cape Town and what she had told him about Mars, about his university application and her telescope and a specific argument about whether optimized cities felt lonelier than unoptimized ones, which they had been having in instalments across six months.  In February he sent her a message that was shorter than usual.  It said: they preserved my grandmother's apartment building. The city resilience program identified it as a heritage structure. The area is part of a managed recovery corridor now — flood-proofed, maintained, monitored. The building where she lived for forty years is safer than it has ever been. The integrated system that would have saved her now also manages the preservation of where she lived.  He wrote: I stood outside it yesterday. I don't know what I'm supposed to feel about this.  She sat with this message for a long time.  She wrote back: I don't know either. I think both things can be true. I think the both-things-being-true is exactly what's hard about it.  He replied: yes. That's exactly what's hard about it.  That was the end of the exchange. Neither of them had anything to add to it. They started a new thread two days later about something else.  She thought about his message sometimes. About the building preserved by the system that should have saved her. About the specific grammar of: the system now manages the place where the cost occurred. About whether this was repair or something that only resembled it.  She didn't resolve it. She wasn't sure it was the kind of thing that resolved.  Later that afternoon she was writing something for the political theory seminar and found herself opening the synthesis recommendation tool before she had formulated her own position on it. She noticed this and closed the tool. She sat for a few minutes with the blankness of not yet knowing what she thought. It was uncomfortable in a specific way — the blankness feeling like something was missing, like a room without the hum she had grown up inside.  She wrote the essay without the tool. It took her longer. The essay was fine. She did not make a note of this anywhere. She was not sure what kind of note it would have been.

She read this three times.

Two weeks before she left, Emilio asked if she wanted to learn to do the weekly crop assessment manually.  She looked at him. It was a Sunday morning and he was in the bay four corridor with the test kit — the physical one, the handheld device with the soil probes and the moisture meter and the small spectrometer for leaf analysis that he had been using every Sunday for twelve years.  The automated system ran daily assessments. The results were in the governance feed. She reviewed them sometimes for her own education. She already knew the methodology — she had read the documentation, she had the theoretical framework, she understood what the measurements were measuring and why.  "Okay," she said.  He showed her how he held the probe — the specific angle, the pressure, how far in, the pause before reading. She replicated it. He watched her do it and made two small corrections and then watched her do it again.  "Your wrist," he said.  "Like this?"  "Less force."  She adjusted. He nodded.  They worked through bay four in the way he had worked through it every Sunday for twelve years: methodically, without hurry, touching each testing point in sequence. She got the readings. They matched the automated system's morning report within the normal variation range.  "They always match," she said.  "Usually."  "When don't they?"  "When the automated sensor has a calibration drift I haven't caught yet. Or when something has changed in the last four hours that the morning report doesn't show." He moved to the next row. "Or when they don't match and I can't explain why and I investigate until I can."  She worked beside him for another twenty minutes. The bay smelled the way it always smelled — the specific humidity and mineral quality that had been the smell of morning for her entire life.  "Why do you still do it?" she asked. Not as a challenge. As a genuine question.  He was quiet for a moment. He finished the reading he was taking before he answered.  "Because I know this bay," he said. "Not the data about it. The thing itself. What it looks like when the second tier is getting enough light. What the leaves feel like when the humidity is slightly off. What the air tastes like when something is wrong before it's registered as wrong."  She considered this. She was standing in a row of tomato plants she had known her entire life — the grow-lights, the humidity, the specific quality of the air — and she realized she knew all of it abstractly and none of it the way he knew it.  "If the sensors fail," she said.  "Then I'm the backup," he said. "But that's not really why I do it."  "Then why?"  He stood up from the row. He looked at the plants. He had the expression he got sometimes — the one she had always associated with him thinking about something he found difficult to say in words rather than actions.  "Because the bay knows I come," he said finally. "That's not a scientific statement. I know it's not a scientific statement. But I have been in this room every Sunday for twelve years, and I think the room is different for it in some way that doesn't show up in the sensors, and I am not going to stop coming because the sensors are good enough."  Paz stood in the humid air of bay four with the test kit in her hand and looked at her father.  He was fifty-four years old and had come to Mars at thirty-one with nothing except competence and intention, and had spent twenty-three years building something that didn't need him, and still came to bay four every Sunday to put his hands in it.  She thought: I grew up knowing the data about this place. He grew up knowing the place.  She thought: those are not the same knowledge, and I don't know yet what I'll lose by only having one of them, and by the time I know it will probably be too late to go back and get the other.  She finished the assessment row. She handed him back the test kit.  "Thank you," she said.  "You can come back for another session if you want," he said. "Before you leave."  She knew she wouldn't. There wasn't time. She also knew, for reasons she would spend the next year trying to articulate, that this had not been about the tomatoes.

* * *

She and Emeka argued about the ocean for three weeks.  She had gone to the coast for a weekend in April — a beach town two hours south, recommended by a classmate — and had stood in front of it for a long time and had found herself unable to decide what she was feeling, which was unusual for her. She described this to Emeka in a message.  He replied: what specifically couldn't you decide?  She wrote: whether it was impressive or just very large. The data tells me it's both but the experience didn't feel like both simultaneously. It felt like something I didn't have the right emotional category for.  He replied: that might be because you're from somewhere that also has extreme scale, but your extreme scale is a landscape that wants to kill you and so you developed a relationship with scale that is specifically different from the relationship people from habitable places developed with it. The ocean to you is just a large benign thing.  She wrote back: that's either insightful or insulting.  He replied: both, probably. Which is also a thing I should work on.  The argument, such as it was, was about whether responses to the natural world were culturally transmitted or experientially acquired or some combination. They went back and forth for three weeks across seven or eight exchanges, pulling in things they'd read, disagreeing about whether a specific paper's methodology was sound, agreeing that another one's conclusion didn't follow from its data. She looked forward to the exchanges in a way that was specific — not like looking forward to information, like looking forward to someone.  She had friends at the university. She had found people she liked, which she had expected to take longer than it did. But the thing with Emeka was different. It was the difference, she thought, between people you found in your immediate context and a person who had specifically become a person to you — whose particular way of thinking had become something you looked for.  She had not had many friendships of this kind. She had grown up in a community of two hundred people where relationships had a specific quality: dense, obligatory, and genuinely close in the particular way of communities where you couldn't not know each other. She had not known what it felt like to choose someone.  She told him this in a message, which was less embarrassing than saying it in a video call.  He replied: I grew up in Lagos with eleven million people and the challenge was the opposite. Choosing someone from the overwhelming supply. You learn to let most people be general. The specific ones are specific.  She replied: yes.  He replied: yes.  The ocean argument concluded, provisionally, that responses to scale were both. They moved on to other things.

* * *

She applied to the university in Cape Town — the one with the political theory program and the view of the mountain — and was accepted in December. She told her parents over dinner in the bay four common area, which was where the family ate when something important happened. Hina cried. Emilio did not cry but had the expression he got when he was managing something. He was a careful man. He had come to Mars at thirty-one with the intention of building something that would last, and he had built it — the agricultural systems running on schedule, the yields within projected parameters, his daughter sitting across the table telling them she was leaving because the place she had been born could not teach her the things she needed to know about itself. He looked at his food. He thought: I raised her here. I thought that would be enough. He did not say this. He was fifty-four years old and had learned, over those years, the difference between what you felt and what you said at a dinner table when your child was already leaving.

"How long?" Hina said.

"Two years. Maybe more."

"Maybe more."

"I don't know yet."

Hina looked at her. She was not, Paz knew, surprised. Hina had been watching her since she was born on this colony and had understood, before Paz understood, that the colony had made her something that would eventually need to know what it had made her in relation to.

"Come back," Hina said.

"Mom."

"I'm not asking you not to go. I'm asking you to come back."

Paz looked at her mother. She thought about the grow-lights in bay four, which had been the first beautiful thing she had known. She thought about Malik and his stone and the fourteen-year photograph on the common-area wall. She thought about Valeria in the operations center reading the morning synthesis, day after day, in a colony that managed itself while she watched.

"Yes," she said. "I'll come back."

Hina nodded. She cleared the plates. She ran the water. Emilio sat at the table until she told him she was fine and he didn't have to stay. He went to their room. She stood at the sink and let the water run warm and thought about the first year — the specific terror of new parenthood in a closed system, the fourteen-minute delay to any doctor, the nights she had lain awake checking the biosensor readout on her phone because she did not trust herself to know if Paz was breathing correctly. The system had always known. The alerts had never fired because the alerts had never needed to fire. The system had managed Paz's environment with a precision Hina could not match, from the first month. She had known this. She had been grateful for it. She turned the water off. She dried her hands. The colony was running its evening cycle. Everything within parameters. She went to bed.
* * *
She called her mother on a Sunday in August, six months after arriving in Cape Town. The call was routine — the Sunday call that had been happening across the fourteen-minute relay delay her entire life, now happening across no delay at all, which should have been better and was, except that the immediacy of it made her aware of things the delay had previously absorbed.  Hina answered quickly. She was in the common area. Bay four growing cycle behind her. The tomatoes on schedule.  They talked for thirty-two minutes. Paz told her about the university. About Emeka and the group she'd found. About the market she had discovered three streets from her apartment, which was loud and unoptimized and which she went to every Saturday for reasons she was still working out.  Hina listened. She asked the right questions. She said the right things.  At minute twenty-four, Paz noticed something. She was not sure at first what she was noticing. She kept talking and noticed it again. And then she understood: her mother was not worried.  Not performing ease. Not suppressing anxiety behind composed questions. Genuinely, specifically not worried. Not about the market, not about Paz navigating a city of four million people alone, not about the places she walked at night, not about any of the things that Paz had, in growing up, understood her mother to carry as a specific kind of vigilance.  The colony's monitoring systems tracked her location. Her biosignals. Her sleep. Her stress indicators, available to family members who had enabled the sharing, which Hina had enabled on the day Paz left. She could see, at any moment, that Paz was fine. She had the data. The data was continuous. The data was correct.  The vigilance had nothing to perform its function on.  They said goodbye warmly. Paz set down her phone and looked out the window at the mountain, which was doing what it always did, which was be there.  She thought about her mother laying awake in year one checking the biosensor readout. About the fourteen-minute relay delay, which had required her mother to send love into a gap and trust that it arrived. About the specific texture of parental worry, which was not rational and was not efficient and had been, for as long as she could remember, one of the ways she knew she was loved.  The monitoring data was accurate. Her mother was not worried. The call had been warm and easy and entirely real.  She sat with this for a long time without deciding what it meant. She thought about Arjun checking the nitrogen at 11 PM. About Miguel reading fiction in the seven minutes. About her father looking at his food and not saying the thing.  She thought: something was maintained here, in these adjustments, in these small irrational continuations, that the word maintenance does not capture.  She could not have said what it was.  She was certain it mattered.

* * *

She went to say goodbye to Valeria on the last day.

Valeria was in the operations center, which was where she was most days. She had been building something in the honest account — Paz had read parts of it, had been given access in year fifteen — and there was the particular quality about her this year of someone finishing something they had been working on for a very long time.

"I'm going to feel rain," Paz said.

"Yes."

"And then I'll understand what you understand, a little more."

Valeria looked at her. "Maybe. You'll also understand things I don't. Because you were born here and I wasn't. You'll have a comparison I can't make."

Paz stood in the doorway of the operations center. The monitoring displays ran their quiet streams behind Valeria. The colony around them managed its forty-three thousand adjustments per hour without effort.

"Are you going home?" Paz said. "Eventually?"

Valeria looked at the displays. She looked at them for a moment in the way she sometimes looked at things — not reading them, just being with them.

"I don't know what home is anymore," she said. "The place I grew up or the place I built something."

"Can it be both?"

"I don't know."

Paz looked at her. Valeria was fifty-two years old and had been on Mars for seventeen years and had not left and might not leave and had built the thing that was now so embedded in the colony's existence that the new arrivals did not experience it as a thing at all. It was just where they lived.

"I'll write to you," Paz said.

"Yes."

"And when I come back, you can tell me if the rain changed anything."

"Only you can tell me that."

"I know. I meant you can listen while I tell you."

Valeria almost smiled. Paz had her mother's precision and her father's loyalty and something else, something that was hers and Mars's, that Valeria had been watching develop for seventeen years and still could not fully name.

"Go," Valeria said.

Paz went.
What the Earth Felt Like

The flight from Johannesburg to Cape Town was two hours. She spent most of it with her face against the window, watching the geometry of the land below — the patterns of roads and cultivation and the uneven sprawl of cities that had grown without optimization, following the logic of water and trade routes and accident and the slow accumulation of human choices that weren't made by any single intelligence at any single moment.

She had studied Earth landscapes. She had seen them in photographs and satellite data and the educational materials that the colony provided about the planet its inhabitants came from. None of this had told her about the specific quality of seeing an unoptimized landscape at altitude — the way it looked genuinely random, in the mathematical sense, without the spatial logic of a designed system. The colony's layouts were visible from above as human intelligence: planned, organized, purposeful. This land below her was human intelligence too, but accumulated over centuries without any single directing intelligence, and it looked different in a way she was not prepared for.

It looked like something had happened to it.

She arrived in March. The mountain was there from the first moment, a fact about the city more permanent than the city itself. She had been told about the mountain. She had seen photographs of it. It was still surprising. Not its size — she had the scale approximately right from the data. The surprise was its indifference. The mountain had no awareness of the city at its base. The city did not affect the mountain. They coexisted in the specific relationship of a small temporary thing and a large permanent thing, which she understood from Mars but had experienced only in relation to the entire planet — not a single landmass feature that could be walked to, that changed as the light changed, that was visible from where she stood and that was not, in any way, there for her.

She stood in the parking lot of the arrivals terminal for eight minutes looking at it.  The next morning she woke in the transit hotel and stood up too fast and had to sit back down. The gravity was 0.38g on Mars. Here it was 1.0. She knew this — had been briefed, had trained, had prepared. Her body knew it differently. Her legs understood the difference between knowing and weighing it. She sat on the edge of the bed and let her legs arrive.  She thought: I have spent my entire life inside a managed interior. This is the first room I have slept in that was not managing out the actual air.  She stood up slowly the second time. Her legs held.

Then she went to get her luggage.
* * *
The rain came in the first week. Not a storm — a Cape Town April rain, light and sideways, coming off the ocean in the specific way of Atlantic weather that didn't commit to being anything in particular.

She stood outside the housing complex in it for twenty-two minutes. She had planned to stand in the first rain she encountered. She had been planning it for two years, since Emeka had told her it came from everywhere at once, and she had wanted to test the description against the experience.

The description was accurate. It came from everywhere at once. It was also cold in a way that was specifically different from the cold of the colony, which was the cold of a managed temperature system maintaining 19 degrees against the exterior — a resistance cold, a cold the system was winning against. This was the cold of water carrying the temperature of the Atlantic Ocean and the air above it and arriving at her body with no particular interest in her. Not hostile. Simply indifferent. The world before the system had been full of this kind of indifference, she thought. The cold that was not directed at you. The rain that would fall regardless.

She thought about the dust spirals, which also fell regardless. But the dust spirals were logged. Someone had designed a system to log them. Even the dust spirals on Mars were documented, were tracked, were part of the data.

This rain was not being logged.

She stood in it until she was uncomfortable, then ten minutes more.
* * *
The market was on Saturdays, in the neighborhood she had chosen to live in for the first year. She went on her first Saturday and lasted forty-five minutes before the sensory load became the kind that required sitting somewhere quiet.

She went back the next Saturday and stayed two hours.

By April she was going every week and staying until the market closed.

What drew her was not what she had expected. She had expected the inefficiency — the redundant vendors selling the same things, the negotiation that could be settled instantly by a price synthesis, the produce that wasn't graded or quality-managed, the general absence of optimization. She had anticipated being intellectually interested in the inefficiency.

What actually drew her was the argument.

People argued in the market. About prices and quality and the correct way to prepare a specific fish and whether a neighbor's produce this week was better than last week's and what had changed in the weather. The arguments were not usually productive in any operational sense. They were the small frictions of people with different information and different preferences and different histories occupying the same space and having to figure out what to do about that.

The colony managed these frictions. She had grown up in an environment where the allocation questions — who got what, when, at what cost — were handled by systems that minimized the friction. The result was a remarkably low-conflict community. She had studied this in the behavioral data. She had not had the comparison case.

The comparison case was people arguing about fish.

She watched this for a long time. Not to draw conclusions. To understand what it was that the colony's efficiency had replaced.

She wrote in her notebook, the Cape Town version of the honest account she had promised Valeria she would eventually write: I think what gets lost in the optimization is the negotiation. Not the arguments about prices. The deeper thing underneath the arguments, which is the ongoing adjustment of people to each other's reality. When the system handles the allocation, people don't have to develop the skill of adjusting to each other. They develop the skill of adjusting to the system's outputs. These produce similar outcomes in the short term. I don't know what they produce over a generation.

She spent one afternoon in March trying to pick an elective course for her second semester. The university's recommendation system offered her three options ranked by fit score based on her interests and academic path. She looked at the list for a while. Then she closed it and tried to decide without it.  She sat with the blank document for twenty minutes. She opened the course catalog and scrolled through it. She closed it. She opened the recommendation list again. She picked the highest-ranked option. It was probably the right choice. She couldn't feel whether it was.  She thought: I have never had to choose something I might regret. The colony managed the options. The school choices were recommended. The food was scheduled. She had been making decisions her entire life inside frameworks that had already eliminated the bad ones. She did not know what it felt like to choose badly. She did not know if that was a loss or not.  She went home. She cooked something. It wasn't very good.  She had been cooking since arriving, which was harder than expected. The colony's food was optimized for nutrition and reliable output. It was not optimized for the experience of assembling components that didn't have obvious relationships to each other. She had burned things. She had oversalted things. She had once produced something that was technically all the listed ingredients in approximately the stated proportions and that she'd still had to throw away.  She cooked every night anyway. The point was something she hadn't expected: cooking was the most completely present she felt in any given day. Not data-present. Present in the way of a task that required her hands and her nose and her attention simultaneously and that would fail in a specific way if she stopped paying that attention.  She ate what she made. It wasn't very good. It was exactly what she wanted.

She ate it and felt satisfied in a way that the colony's nutritionally optimized food had never produced, which was probably not nutritional and was entirely real.
* * *
The call to Hina in August was the one that stayed with her.

Thirty-two minutes of conversation that was warm and ordinary. Her mother not worried. The colony's monitoring data providing the reassurance that worry had previously provided.

She set down the phone and looked at the mountain through the window.

She thought about the specific quality of being loved by someone who was worried about you. Not the worry itself — she did not miss being the cause of anxiety. But the worry had been, she understood now for the first time, a form of contact. The anxious attention of her mother checking the biosensor readout at 3 AM in year one had been a form of presence. A form of the relationship.

The system now provided the function the worry had served. Better. More accurately. Without the cost to her mother's sleep.

Something had also disappeared.

She did not know what to call it. She wrote in the notebook: there is a form of love that consists of being the source of someone's worry. Not because you want them to suffer. Because worry is one of the ways the body produces the state of caring about something. You don't worry about things you're indifferent to. My mother worried about me because she loved me and because I was fragile and because she was the thing standing between me and the fragility. The system is more reliable at that function than she was. It has also made her indifferent in a way she wouldn't describe as indifference. She loves me. She trusts the data. These are not in conflict. I don't know what they replace.

She sat with this for a long time. The mountain was in the window, doing what mountains did.

She thought: I came here to understand what the colony made me, in relation to a world it wasn't part of. I have been here for five months. I am beginning to understand some things. I don't have words for all of them yet.

She picked up her notebook and kept writing.
 

Valeria turned back to the operations displays. The synthesis was compiling for the afternoon cycle. The colony was managing its systems. Bay three was on schedule. The tomato plants were growing.

She opened the honest account. She wrote the last entry of year seventeen.

Then she closed it.

She sat in the operations center for a while, in the quiet hum of a civilization managing itself, and let herself feel whatever it was she was feeling, without trying to name it yet.  Somewhere in the colony, someone was sleeping who had not slept well the night before. The monitoring system had noted the sleep quality. An adjustment had been made to the temperature in their module. The adjustment had worked. The person did not know any of this. They slept.  The synthesis continued compiling. Bay three was on schedule. The tomato plants were growing. The photograph of Leila Nasser was on the wall in the common area, recently replaced, where it had been for nine years and where it would continue to be after everyone who had known her had left the colony.  The colony did not know that Valeria Solis was leaving in the morning. The colony was a system. Systems did not know about departures.  This was, she thought, correct.  It was also the loneliest thing she had ever built.
* * *

She arrived in Cape Town on a Wednesday in March.  The flight from Johannesburg was two hours. She had been in transit for six days, counting the shuttle from Mars, and her body had not yet completed its adjustment to Earth gravity, which was heavier than she remembered and also exactly what she remembered, the two things existing simultaneously and being somehow both true.  The city was visible from the descent. She had seen photographs. She had prepared herself. The photographs had not captured the specific color of the ocean at that altitude — a blue that was not the blue in images, that was the blue of an actual very large amount of water in actual direct light, which was different in a way she found she did not have the right category for.  The mountain was there before she expected it. She had thought: the mountain is behind the city. The mountain was there when she looked for the city. It had not arranged itself to be convenient. It was simply in the landscape the way Mars was in its landscape — indifferent to whether she was looking or not, ancient beyond the relevance of that distinction.  She sat in the arrivals terminal for eleven minutes. She had allowed herself to do this. Seventeen years on Mars had produced, among other things, a detailed understanding of when she needed to sit quietly before the next thing. She sat. The terminal was noisy and well-managed and entirely beneath the mountain’s awareness.  A message had arrived from Malik, routed through the relay with the usual lag now removed: the stone from sector eleven had made the trip well, he had carried it to the house in Monterrey, Elena said to tell her she hoped Valeria had stood in the rain for a long time. He said: we were both protecting ourselves. That seems about right. Take care of yourself on the other side of all of it.  She read this in the arrivals terminal under the mountain. She did not reply immediately. She would reply later, in the right words, when she had found them. She would probably never fully find them.  She had also received, three days after leaving Mars, a letter from Elena. Not a message. A letter, typed and formatted and attached as a document, in the specific form Elena used when she wanted something to exist as an artifact rather than a communication. She had read it twice on the transit shuttle and had not read it since.  Elena’s letter said: I read the year-two routing documentation again last month. I kept a copy on my personal archive.  I read it again because I wanted to understand something. What I wanted to understand was: did you know? Not whether you built it deliberately. I know you didn’t. I mean: did you know, in the way that the body knows things before the mind does, that what you were building was also a reason?  I think you did. I think the routing system is the most honest thing you ever made.  The garden here has a fig tree that produces every September. Malik says it was here before us. The tomatoes are irregular and sometimes split and taste like something happened to them.  Come home, Valeria. Not to Mars. To wherever it is.  She was in Cape Town. This was, she thought, one answer to that.  She picked up her bag and went to get the rental, in the rain, which was exactly as cold and sideways and indifferent as the rain had always been on every planet she had not grown up on.

I know that it's different. That civilization has changed shape, and that I was one of the people who changed it, and that I will carry that for the rest of my life without being able to say with confidence whether what I carried it toward was better.

This book is not an argument.

It is a record. Of what happened, as accurately as I can reconstruct it. Of what was gained and what was lost, as honestly as I can say it. Of a transition that is still in progress and that will be understood more clearly by the people who come after than by the people who lived through it.

I hope they are generous.

I hope they find it was worth it.

I hope they remember to ask whether it was.  I hope the asking is not too easy by then.

Author's Note

The events described in this novel are fictional. The governance gap they describe is not.

NUBELAI is not a prediction. It is the name I've given to what fills the gap when the gap becomes intolerable — whether we fill it deliberately, with oversight and accountability and democratic engagement, or whether operational necessity fills it for us.

The question the novel asks is not whether that filling will happen. The question is what we will have built into it when it does. Whether we will have been honest about what it cost. Whether anyone will remain who remembers the cost and is willing to say so.
