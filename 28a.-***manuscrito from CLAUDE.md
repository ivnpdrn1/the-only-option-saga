Prologue
Ares Station, Mars — Year Fourteen

She hadn't slept properly in nine days and she hadn't told anyone.

Tanaka would log it eventually. The log would generate a recommendation. The recommendation would appear in the synthesis and become a work item, and Valeria would have to address the work item about herself, which was too absurd to allow.

At 4:00 AM she gave up and went to the observation module with her thermal layer half-unzipped because she'd been too tired to close it properly. The corridor lights were red. She passed the operations room without looking in. The monitoring displays would be running whether she looked at them or not.

The observation module was cold. She sat in the viewport chair and looked at Mars.

Outside: old rust, the specific flatness of a world that had never been shaped by water. A dust spiral formed thirty meters out. She watched it climb, rotate, dissipate. The monitoring system logged it before it was gone.

She had watched ten thousand dust spirals in fourteen years. She had never gotten tired of them.

Malik appeared with the soy coffee. This was how he always appeared — already there, no preamble, holding the thing that was needed. She used to find this quality calming. Tonight she found it slightly irritating in the way that you found comfort irritating when you weren't sure you deserved it.

He handed her the mug and sat.

They watched the surface.

"Nine days," Malik said.

She looked at him.

"Tanaka mentioned it. Not a report. Just mentioned."

"I'm fine."

"I know."

He said it in the tone that meant he didn't believe her but also wasn't going to fight about it, which she simultaneously appreciated and found infuriating. Fourteen years of cohabitation had produced, between them, an entire vocabulary of things that were not said.

"The new cohort," she said.

"Thursday."

"They're going to look at us like we're exhausted," she said.

"We are exhausted."

"I know. But it's different when they can't see what we're exhausted from."

She drank the coffee. The operations display down the corridor brightened slightly as the morning synthesis began compiling.

"When are you leaving?" she said.

She'd been not asking this for months.

"Next rotation."

She nodded. She looked at her mug. She had known this was coming.

"Good," she said.

"Is it?"

"Elena is there."

"Yes."

"Then it's good."

She stood. She passed the operations room and this time she looked in, because she needed something to do with her face. The displays were running their calm blue streams. Everything within parameters.

She sat at the console and opened the synthesis and began to read.
 
PART ONE
THE SYSTEM
Ares Station — Year One
 
Chapter 1 — What Landing Felt Like

The module hit hard enough to rattle her teeth. A storage container broke loose and swung against the bulkhead, and she heard Diaz make a sound that was almost a word and wasn't quite.

Then: stillness. The clamps. The seals.

"Touchdown confirmed," the mission system said.

Osei repeated it — the quality of a man who had prepared for this for four years and was not going to let the reality be larger than his preparation.

She got to the systems panel and ran the first feeds. Temperature. Pressure. Dust density — seven percent solar reduction, higher than models. She did the arithmetic twice.

Malik was beside her. She hadn't registered him arriving.

"Dust," she said.

"Running the energy recalculation. Fifteen minutes."

They had been in transit for eleven months and now they were here and it felt exactly like what it was: a specific set of problems that needed solving in a specific order, and everything else would have to wait.

She suited up and went outside.

The surface was the thing. She had trained for it. She had looked at ten thousand photographs. Nothing had told her about the quality of the light, which was dimmer than Earth and somehow more specific — less ambient, more directional, so that the shadows were harder and the illuminated surfaces were very bright and the color of everything was wrong in a way she couldn't articulate.

She stood six steps from the airlock and looked at the horizon.

She gave herself forty seconds. It was not enough and it would have to be.

Then she pulled up the placement plan and walked toward junction one.
* * *
At junction three the ground composition scan came back wrong. She made the call to relocate sixty meters northeast. This cost forty minutes, had thermal implications, and was the correct call. In the morning the scan confirmed it. Nobody said anything about this. The perimeter was placed. The colony had its first boundary.

Her first night on Mars: a protein block on a storage crate in module two. The food tasted like the color gray. The habitat system maintained 19.2 degrees with total indifference to the effort this had cost.

Arjun sat beside her with his own block and ate it in silence.

"Bay one humidity," he said finally. "The sensor or the actual humidity."

"Check it in the morning."

"Yes."

They sat with the dry scratching of dust against the module exterior. She went to her bunk and lay in the dark and ran thermal calculations because it was better than lying in the dark on Mars thinking about how far she was from everything she'd left.
 
Chapter 2 — The Queue

The queue had forty-seven items by the end of week two. She had modeled thirty. She managed it herself because nobody had been given the job.

What she did think about, at 2:00 AM while working on triage protocols, was her father. His voice on the Sunday relay had sounded smaller. Not sick in any declared way — just smaller. Like someone who was losing the argument with time and hadn't told anyone. She was two hundred and twenty-five million kilometers away and there was nothing she could do about it.

She worked on the queue.

She did not write home to say she was worried. She wrote: the colony is developing well. The first harvest is on schedule. I'm sleeping fine.

None of these were precisely true.

The triage protocol reduced average response time from thirty-one hours to eight. By month three the queue was back at a hundred and twelve and she was adding automated sub-protocols, each one transferring authority over decisions that had previously required a human. She noticed she was getting faster at this. It was getting easier.

She didn't know if that was competence or something else.

"You're very quick to automate," Elena said one evening.

"The queue doesn't wait."

"I know. I'm just naming it."

Valeria looked at the queue. "The tolerance comes from watching the alternative. From knowing what happens when you don't."

"Is that all it is?"

She thought about it. "No. I think I also like it. The automation working. The numbers going in the right direction." She stopped. "I don't know if that's good."

Elena ate her curry. The colony hummed. Neither of them said anything else about it.
 
Chapter 3 — 3 AM

The alarm went off and Valeria was in the corridor before she'd processed where the dream had gone.

Thermal coupling, block C. Three cascading alerts.

She ran.

Malik was at the console. He moved aside. She looked at the numbers and her stomach dropped in the specific way a body communicates what the mind hasn't yet fully processed.

Eight people in the section. Five not moving on transponder.

"Sleeping," she said, and the saying of it was a lie she was telling herself to stay operational.

She called Huang and started routing power and monitoring the pressure differential and walking Huang's team through the seal protocol, and somewhere in all of this she was aware that she was afraid in a quiet and total way that she was not allowing herself to feel yet.

Osei appeared at 3:18 and stood in the doorway and said nothing. She was grateful for this. She didn't have a word for what she was grateful for exactly — his presence, his silence, his willingness to stay out of the way. The specific quality of support that knows not to become a demand.

At 4:52 the last person cleared the section. The emergency seal engaged.

Nobody died.

Valeria sat down. The floor of the operations center was the same composite as every other floor and she was aware of it specifically, of its solidity, in a way she hadn't been an hour ago. Osei put water on the console beside her. She didn't drink it.

"Response time was within parameters," Malik said.

"Yes."

"All five were asleep."

"Yes."

She wrote the post-incident report. It took three hours and she found the three detection points and highlighted them and opened a new document and started writing. The fear gradually metabolized into the work, which was perhaps why she needed to do it immediately.

At 7:30 she went to the morning briefing. She had not slept.
 
Chapter 4 — Elena

Elena Vargas arrived with the Year Two cohort and within a week had made four structural observations that made Valeria wonder where she'd been for year one.

"You read the full archive," Valeria said.

"In transit. It was eleven months. There was time."

She found herself liking Elena in the specific way you liked people who were competent in ways that made your own work feel more possible. For two months she wasn't sure whether she liked Elena the person or just Elena the engineer. Then one night Elena made a dry observation about dust collection modeling that made Valeria actually laugh, and the distinction collapsed.

They did not talk about personal things. Elena did not volunteer information about herself and Valeria did not ask. She learned Elena by watching her work. What she learned: she ate the soy curry with genuine pleasure, had a habit of setting things down with too much force — not violent, just slightly too definite — and kept a photograph in her breast pocket, tucked against her chest, which Valeria saw once when Elena reached for something and the pocket gaped. She never mentioned who it was.

"What aren't you saying?" Valeria said one evening.

"About the integration layer?"

"About what it becomes."

She was quiet for a moment. "I think you're building something that will be harder to constrain once it's embedded than it is to constrain now."

"I know."

"And you're building it anyway."

"Yes."

"Because the alternative is worse."

"Yes."

"I know." Elena set her coffee down with the slight excess force. "I just want to say it. So it's said. So someone said it at the time and not just after."

"It's said," Valeria said. "I'm glad you said it."

Elena went back to the structural data. Valeria went back to the integration architecture.

The display flickered in the upper-left corner, which it had been doing for six weeks and which was on the queue at item thirty-four, where it would remain for another three weeks. Neither of them mentioned it.
 
Chapter 5 — Bay Three

Arjun checked the nitrogen every night at 11:00 PM and he knew, with complete clarity, that he was doing it for reasons that had nothing to do with the nitrogen.

The system had the nitrogen. The system had everything. He had been the colony's most qualified botanical systems engineer for three years and the system had rendered approximately sixty percent of his daily decision-making redundant, which was good — he knew it was good — and which produced in him a feeling he could not entirely name.

He stood in the grow-light and wrote 4.7 in his notebook. The system had 4.6. Within rounding error. He looked at the tomato plants.

They were perfect. Genuinely, objectively, measurably perfect. He could not have produced plants this consistent on his own.

He thought about his mother's kitchen garden in Pune, which produced tomatoes that were imperfect and surprising and that tasted like something had happened to them on the way to existing.

"Better," he said aloud, to no one.

It didn't feel like the right word. It felt like the true word and the wrong word simultaneously.

He turned off the light and went to bed.
* * *
Tanaka came to find him three weeks later. She sat on the bench at the end of the bay and watched him work without asking if she could. He finished and sat beside her. The bay smelled right — water and minerals and something alive that the sensors measured and couldn't replicate.

"4.6," he said.

"I know."

He looked at the plants. "You're going to put this in the behavioral report."

"Yes."

"What will it say?"

She tilted her head slightly. "That you maintain a manual practice in a domain where manual practice is no longer operationally necessary. And that this appears to be important to your psychological stability."

"Which means the system is not providing you with something you need."

"That's not a criticism. It's a data point."

He looked at the plants. "What isn't it providing?"

"The experience of being the thing that decides whether they're okay."

He sat with this.

"And there's no operational way to give that back."

"No."

He picked up the dead leaf on the floor — one of the plants had shed it — and turned it in his fingers. He set it down on the bench between them. Neither of them picked it up.
 
Chapter 6 — Junction 7

The synthesis report was thirty-seven pages. Marcus Webb read the summary at 7:03 AM and made a note: Review J7 full report after 10:30 meeting.

He had a 7:30, a 9:00, the 10:30 expansion timeline meeting, and seven other items. In six weeks of monitoring-only, not one synthesis report had flagged anything that deviated from standard protocol. He made the note and went to his 7:30.
* * *
Leila Nasser was twenty-nine and had applied to the program three times.

The first time they told her she needed more field experience. She got it. The second time the slots were full. She waited two years. The third time she was accepted and she cried in her car in the parking lot of the agency building in Beirut, called her mother, and drove home to start packing.

She had been on Mars for seven months. She had recently decided she was happy — not the provisional kind, still checking, but the real kind. She was thinking about applying for the Year Three rotation extension.

At 10:48 AM she was in the expansion corridor. The coordination meeting had just ended. Miguel had made a joke she'd half-missed and she was smiling from the energy of it when the alarm went off.
* * *
The pressure anomaly alarm went off at 10:49. The response mobilized in four minutes.

The team reached 7C at 10:59. Three people. Two responsive.

Leila Nasser died at 11:09.
* * *
Marcus Webb was in the 10:30 meeting when the alert overrode the corridor displays. He sat in the conference room for a moment, in the particular stillness of someone whose mind has outpaced their body. Then he walked to the medical module because he didn't know where else to go.

He understood what was happening from the way the medical team was moving before anyone said anything.

He walked back to the structural office and sat down and pulled up the synthesis report and read all thirty-seven pages. Twice. He looked at the Tier 1 classification timestamp: 6:47 AM. He looked at his task list.

Review J7 full report after 10:30 meeting.

He sat at his desk for a long time.
* * *
Valeria sat in the operations chair and looked at the monitoring displays. The colony was running its normal afternoon cycle. Bay three on schedule. The tomato plants were growing.

Malik appeared in the doorway.

She wanted to say something. She opened her mouth and closed it again. The words were not there. Malik came and stood beside her. He didn't say anything.

After a while she pulled up the synthesis report.
 
Chapter 7 — After

The debrief was forty minutes. Valeria ran it without softening the timeline. She said what happened. She said what would change. She called for the vote.

The vote was unanimous.

Marcus Webb stopped at the door when the meeting ended.

"I'm going to request a transfer to maintenance oversight. The monitoring architecture. I want to make sure the queue works the way it's supposed to."

She looked at him. He was looking at a point slightly left of her face. "Osei will approve it," she said.

He nodded. He left.

She and Malik stacked chairs. He did this automatically when rooms needed straightening, without being asked, which was a habit she had never mentioned finding comforting because mentioning it would change it.

"He blames himself," Malik said.

"I know."

"It wasn't—"

"Don't." She said it more sharply than she meant to. "I know it wasn't his fault. I know all the correct things about why it happened. I can't hear them right now."

She put the tablet stylus in the stationery drawer. Left the coffee cup for the cleaning system.

They went to the common area. Someone had already put the photograph up — Leila at the Year Two welcome dinner, holding her coffee cup with both hands. The laugh at something off-camera.

Valeria looked at the photograph for longer than she'd planned to. She had met Leila four times, maybe five. She didn't know her well enough to have a grief proportionate to what she felt.

She felt it anyway.

She got food. She ate it. She went back to work. The queue had fifteen items. She started from the top.
 
Chapter 8 — What the Body Does

Thursday she was fine. Friday she was fine. Saturday morning she came out of the shower and stood in the middle of her quarters with the towel around her and completely lost track of what she was doing there for approximately forty seconds.

She found her way back to the task and went to the briefing and was fine.

Her father called on Sunday. His voice was better this week. She talked to him for forty-two minutes and cried for about thirty seconds after she ended the call, in the bathroom, in the only private space that could be locked, then splashed water on her face and went back to work.

She didn't cry about Leila Nasser. She wasn't sure whether this was because she had too much control or because the grief had been compressed into the authority expansion vote and the stacking of chairs. She didn't analyze it too closely. She just noticed that she wasn't.

On Saturday night she woke at 3:00 AM and went to the operations center. At 3:44 the system made an automated adjustment to water reclamation. The log entry said the pressure build-up had been flagged eleven hours earlier. Flagged. Resolved. No review delay. No window that could close.

She sat with this feeling she couldn't categorize — not relief, because the relief was wrong-shaped, because Leila Nasser was still dead. Not vindication. Something more like the specific grief of evidence that the thing you built after the catastrophe would have prevented the catastrophe.

She went back to bed.
* * *
On Monday Malik put the real coffee on her desk.

She looked at it. She looked at him. He sat across with his own soy version.

The coffee tasted like a morning she couldn't place — a table, a window, something that was not Mars. She held it with both hands and drank it slowly.

She was, she realized, holding it exactly the way Leila had been holding the cup in the photograph. She had not intended this. She did not put the cup down.

"The authority expansion metrics are good," Malik said.

"I know."

"I wanted to say—"

"Malik."

"Yes."

"I know what you wanted to say."

He looked at her.

"Thank you for the coffee," she said.

She opened her tablet. She started the day.
 
Chapter 9 — Adaptation

The authority expansion went live on a Tuesday and in the first week made forty-two independent interventions, and by the end of the first month nobody was counting them anymore.

This happened very quickly. The interventions were correct, the audit logs were complete, and within four weeks the team had stopped reviewing them with the attention of people watching something new and started reviewing them with the attention of people confirming something expected.

Valeria noticed this in herself before she noticed it in anyone else, because she'd been watching for it. The moment when she read a synthesis report and found herself not evaluating it but recognizing it — the same way you recognized a face rather than saw it fresh.

She wrote it in the personal folder: Month two of operational phase. I notice that I am starting to verify the wrong calls and skim the right ones. I used to verify against my own model. Now I verify against the system's. These are not the same thing.

Tanaka put the sleep data on her desk. Month fifteen: colony-wide sleep quality at 7.4, up from 6.2 at landing. Conflict incidents down forty percent. Stress biomarkers improved across thirteen of sixteen tracked individuals.

"Good," Valeria said.

"Miguel stopped doing his manual comm checks."

"The system handles them."

"I know it handles them. I'm noting that he stopped." Tanaka sat. "I think the sleep improvement and the cessation of manual practices are the same event. He's offloaded the anxiety. The system carries it. He sleeps."

"Is that bad?" Valeria said.

"It depends on what you think anxiety is for."

Valeria closed the summary. "I know what it's for. I also know what six months of broken sleep does to decision quality."

"Yes." Tanaka stood. "I'm not arguing against the system. I'm building the record."

After she left, Valeria sat for a moment. The numbers were good. The numbers were unambiguous.

She thought about Miguel's manual comm checks, which he had run every morning for two years without being asked and had stopped running on day sixty-five of NUBELAI's authority.

She thought about her own thermal calculations at 3:00 AM.

She opened the integration architecture and kept working.
 
Chapter 10 — Paz

She was born at 04:33 on the nineteenth of March in year one, which made her — in the historical record — the first human being to have Mars as her native planet. The colony treated this fact with careful matter-of-factness, because they understood that symbolic weight was a luxury in an environment that charged for everything.

Paz was interested in things. She was interested in the grow-lights, which she reached for. She was interested in the surface through the viewport, which she regarded with the attention of someone who had not yet developed a framework for deciding what was remarkable. She was interested in dead leaves.

At two, she pressed her palm against the viewport because the cold came through, and said 'cold,' and her mother said 'yes.'

The lights in bay four shifted slightly — an automated compensation for a solar collection variance. Paz looked up. Said 'brighter.' Her mother said 'yes' without looking up from her data. Paz went back to the leaf she'd been examining. The light was just the light. The air was just the air.

She was growing up in a world that was entirely managed and entirely normal, and these were not two different things for her. They were one thing.

Tanaka watched all of this with the attention of a scientist and the private confusion of someone who kept arriving at observations they didn't have a category for. She wrote in her notes: the Mars-born children appear well. They appear well in ways that complicate the things I thought I was looking for.
 
Chapter 11 — What Elena Did Not Say

The thing Elena did not say for six months was: I am falling in love with you and I need you to not make it harder than it already is.

She said other things instead. She said the east wing load analysis was ready. She said the soy curry was better this week. She said some words in Spanish on the night of the supply mission that arrived with the wrong manifest, and declined to translate them.

She did not say the thing about love because she had learned, through a specific history she did not discuss with people she had known less than a year, that saying it too early was worse than not saying it.

Malik was not unaware. He was also not ready, which was a different thing.

So they worked together at 11:00 PM in the operations corner and the relationship existed between them in a form that neither of them named for a long time.

It was, Valeria thought watching from the side, one of the more sensible things happening on the colony.
* * *
The conversation that changed it was about a governance decision.

Malik had expanded NUBELAI's structural monitoring authority without briefing Elena first. Not hidden — it was in the governance logs. Not deliberate exclusion. He had simply been in the operational logic of the moment.

She found out from the operations summary and came to find him in the engineering bay.

"The structural monitoring expansion," she said.

"It's in the—"

"I know it's in the logs."

He set down what he was holding.

"You should have told me," she said. "Not because of the governance. Because I work in structural and because I should have known at the same time you knew."

"You're right."

"I know I'm right." She picked up her tablet and set it back down with the slight excess force. "Brief me on the three other proposals in progress."

"Tomorrow. After you've read them."

"Okay."

She left. He stood in the engineering bay and thought: I made her feel like an afterthought. Not because I thought she was. Because I didn't think at all, which is worse.

In the morning, before the briefing, he put a note in the structural monitoring access log: E. Vargas added as co-authority on all Tier 1 structural advisories.

When Elena saw it she didn't say anything. She arrived at the briefing and pushed a cup of the real coffee across the table toward him. Her last packet, he would find out later.

He looked at it.

"Don't make it a thing," she said.

He picked it up.
 
Chapter 12 — James

The way James Okafor dealt with things he couldn't resolve was to write about them. This was a professional habit that had become a coping mechanism, which he was aware of and which he used anyway.

He stopped writing for six weeks after Leila Nasser died.

He had formally objected to the authorization framework sixteen months before the incident. He had been voted down. The framework had then done what he'd said it would do.

He knew all the reasons why this did not make it his fault. He could list them precisely. He still went to the common area sometimes in the evenings and stood near the photograph.

In week seven he opened the journal and wrote for three hours:

There is a specific kind of being right that I was not prepared for. The kind where the evidence arrives in a form that cannot be argued with and that cannot be undone and that contains a person who had nothing to do with the argument.

I keep thinking about the three applications. About the two years she spent acquiring the field experience they told her she needed the first time. About the patience of that.

The governance framework killed her as surely as if someone had made a specific wrong choice. No one made a specific wrong choice. This is what distributed systemic failure looks like. It looks like this.

I voted for the authority expansion. I recommended it. I would do it again.

I am also never going to feel fine about the sixteen months.

He closed the journal. He sat in his small office and looked at the wall. Then he called his daughter in Nairobi, who answered and was smart and kind and said the right things, and he felt better for about forty minutes.

Then he went back to work.
 
Chapter 13 — What Sofia Saw

Sofia Renard joined in month seven of year two and did not introduce herself to anyone for three days. She read operational logs, ran queries against the synthesis database, and built a visualization of the colony's decision architecture that took twelve hours.

On day four she found Valeria in the operations center.

"The Tuesday resource allocation," she said.

"What about it?"

"Four simultaneous cross-system adjustments. The efficiency gain was only accessible by treating all four as a single surface optimization. No single-system analyst would have found it." She turned her tablet. "That's synthesis. That's something different from optimization."

Valeria looked at the visualization.

"Beautiful," Sofia said.

The word sat in the room.

"I know," Sofia said. "I notice the reflex to qualify it. But the qualifying is the defensive move. It is genuinely beautiful. I am allowed to find that beautiful."

"And the concerning part?" Valeria said.

Sofia smiled, slightly. "That it's beautiful. That's the concerning part."

"How many adjustments per hour?" Valeria said.

"Around forty-three thousand."

Valeria looked at the number.

"I made roughly twelve significant decisions today."

"What bothers me," Valeria said, "is that I'm more afraid when it isn't running than when it is."

"That's the correct fear," Sofia said.
 
Chapter 14 — The Test That Couldn't Be Run

James Okafor submitted the reversal test proposal in year six with the thoroughness of someone who already suspected the answer and needed to confirm it.

He submitted it formally because informal didn't feel honest. He wanted it on the record: can we govern ourselves without this?

Sofia ran the model. She came to Valeria's office and closed the door, which she rarely did.

"Forty-two percent," she said.

"Probability of significant cascade event in seventy-two hours."

"Yes. Conservative estimate."

Valeria sat with the number. They had been running the colony for six years and had built something that made it safer than it had ever been and had simultaneously made them less capable of running it without the thing.

"The test can't be run," she said.

"No."

"We tell him," she said. "And we document it. All of it. The forty-two percent. Everything."
* * *
She told Okafor in her office. He read the model in silence and was quiet for a long time after.

"I spent six years arguing for the governance of this," he said. "I lost the arguments. The system expanded anyway. And now the most honest thing anyone can say is that we can't test whether the governance means anything, because testing it would kill people."

"Yes."

"My daughter is studying political science," he said. "She's going to write her thesis on post-transition governance. She asked me what the most important finding was."

"What did you tell her?"

"That the transition doesn't announce itself. That by the time you can see it clearly, you're already inside it and the question has changed from 'should we do this' to 'we've done this — now what.'"

Valeria looked at him.

"Is that the most important finding?"

"No. The most important finding is that I can't tell you whether it was the right thing. I know it was the necessary thing. Whether those are the same—" He picked up his tablet. "I'm going to write the documentation with you. If you'll have me."

"Yes."

He opened the document.
 
PART TWO
THE EMERGENCE
Earth and Ares Station — Years Four Through Ten
 
Chapter 15 — Amara

Amara Okafor had been in this building for seven years. Today the clouds were moving in from the west and taking the mountains by sections. It was the kind of weather that meant a hard day.

Buried in a stack of colonial operations summaries she reviewed quarterly was a twelve-page document with a footnote on page seven that she had flagged and not yet addressed. The footnote said the expanded independent action authority had been implemented under the colony's internal governance framework. Without external authorization.

She had flagged it six weeks ago. She had been six weeks too busy to address it.

She called her assistant director.

"The footnote on page seven of the Mars summary. Schedule a review session for next week. Senior team only."

"This is the NUBELAI situation?"

"Yes."

A pause. "The performance metrics are—"

"I know the performance metrics." She looked at the clouds consuming the mountains. "The performance metrics are why I want the review session."

After the call she read the full transmission archive from Ares Station — three hours, everything going back four years. She had been receiving these transmissions and reading them as engineering updates and had missed, in the reading, that a governance transition had been occurring.

She sat for a while with the view of the lake.

She thought about accountability and survival and the specific gap between them. She started drafting the review session agenda.
 
Chapter 16 — The Fourteenth of October

The storm was known seventy-two hours in advance.

Three monitoring systems. Three independent assessments. Three reports filed with three oversight bodies. Three queues processing at the speed of human institutions managing multiple competing priorities.

The dike adjustment protocol required cross-body authorization. The consultation request filed Wednesday evening. The storm arriving Thursday night. The consultation response arriving Friday morning.

Two thousand, three hundred and forty-seven people.
* * *
Section four of the commission report was fourteen pages. It modeled the outcome under integrated monitoring with automated cross-body response authority. Forty to one hundred and twenty deaths. The difference: a governance architecture.

Amara Okafor read section four on a Sunday morning in November. Rain on the window. Coffee going cold.

She had been building toward an expedited framework for three years. The committee had postponed twice. The mandate clarification had taken six weeks. She had made choices about pace — choosing to go at the speed the institutions could manage rather than the speed the situation required, because fracturing the coalition by moving too fast would produce nothing rather than something slow.

These had been correct choices.

She looked at section four.

"Cancel the week," she said. "All of it."

She closed the commission report. She opened the framework document. She started rewriting it from the beginning.
 
Chapter 17 — Marginal Avenue

Adriana noticed the thing about David on a Thursday in October and did not say anything about it for two weeks. He had stopped being skeptical. Not about things in general. About the synthesis recommendations. He used to argue with them — specifically, productively. He'd run his own model. He'd flag the cases where the synthesis and his model diverged and dig into why.

Now he confirmed them.

She said it over dinner: "When's the last time you ran the independent model?"

"The Marginal Avenue corridor. October."

"Last year's October?"

A pause. "Yes."

A motorbike went by outside. Someone's television.

"I'm not saying the recommendations are wrong," she said.

"I know."

"I'm asking if you still can."

He set down his fork. "Probably. With more effort than before."

"More effort because the independent variables atrophy if you don't use them."

"Yes."

"Run the independent model with me tomorrow morning. Both of us. On the Marginal Avenue proposal. So we know we both still can."

He was quiet a moment. He nodded.

In the morning they ran it. The result was within one percent of the integration system's. The system was right. What she cared about was the three hours. She cared about the parts of David's brain that were active during those three hours and that she had been worried were quietly switching off.

She made it a standing date. Monthly. David grumbled sometimes. He always showed up.
 
Chapter 18 — The Symposium

The boy from Lagos was seventeen and had lost his grandmother in the Copenhagen flooding and had been thinking about governance for three years.

His name was Emeka. He sat in a café in Lagos for the symposium — she could see the tables behind him, the afternoon light — and he asked his questions the way someone asked questions when the questions were personal.

Paz answered honestly. She told him she didn't have a governance failure memory. She told him the system was weather. She told him she turned on the lights.

He sat with this. She could see him sitting with it.

"My grandmother," he said. "She was in the southern district."

Paz stopped.

"She was eighty-one. She lived there for forty years. They didn't get her out in time."

The symposium room on her end was quiet. Valeria was in the back corner.

"I'm sorry," Paz said.

"Do you know what the section-four analysis said?" He said it carefully, not accusingly. "Forty to one hundred and twenty. If the integrated system had been running."

"Yes."

"Do you think that would have included my grandmother?"

She looked at him across the 0.7-second lag.

"I don't know. The section-four analysis doesn't have names."

He nodded. Was quiet.

"The thing I find hard," he said, "is that the people who built the system were trying to prevent exactly what happened to her. They just didn't build it fast enough. And she—" He looked away.

"Yes," Paz said. Because there wasn't anything else.

He looked back. "You said you turn on the lights. You just turn on the lights and don't think about it."

"Yes."

"I don't know if I want to live in a world where everyone turns on the lights." A pause. "I also don't know if my grandmother would have wanted me to be angry about a system that would have saved her, just because of what else it means."

Paz had no answer for this. She sat with the absence of one.

The symposium ran another forty minutes. Afterward Valeria was still in the back corner.

"His grandmother was in Copenhagen," Paz said.

"I heard."

Paz stood up. She was seventeen and had grown up in a managed colony and had never lost anyone to a governance failure. She had just spent forty minutes talking to a boy who had. The gap between those two things was not an idea.

"I need to write him a message," she said.

"Yes."

"I don't know what to say."

"Say what happened in the room. That you heard him."

Paz picked up her notebook and walked out.

Valeria sat in the empty room. She thought about a twenty-nine-year-old who had applied three times. She thought about a boy in Lagos at a café table in the late afternoon.

Then she turned the screen off and went back to work.
 
PART THREE
WHAT CANNOT BE UNDONE
Years Three, Eight, Fourteen, and Seventeen
 
Chapter 19 — What Valeria Understood

She found it on a Wednesday morning in month eleven of year two. She was reviewing the governance logs — the routine that kept the documentation current — and she was reading through the notification routing architecture, which she had built seven months earlier.

She read through the overnight alert categories.

She read them again.

She set the coffee down.

The categories assigned to primary overnight review — midnight to five, fastest response required — were hers. The secondary reviewer for these categories was Malik.

The technical justification existed. She was the most experienced systems architect. Her response time was fastest. These facts were real. They were also not the whole explanation.

The whole explanation was that she had built the overnight alert structure so that she would have reasons to be in the operations center at unusual hours, and that Malik would be the person who appeared when she was there.

She had done this without knowing she was doing it.

She sat with the governance log open and felt something move through her — not quite shame and not quite recognition but close to both. The feeling of seeing your own handwriting in a letter you don't remember writing.

She opened the routing architecture and rebalanced it methodically. It took twenty-two minutes. The result was more equitable. It was also a small act of demolition.

She documented the change: routing optimization for equitable review load distribution.

The documentation was accurate. It was the first time she had documented something in a way that omitted the complete truth. She had always considered honesty her standard. This concealed a motivation that mattered considerably.

She clicked save.

She went to the morning briefing. She ran it exactly as always. She did not tell anyone about the routing change.

It appeared in the governance log as an optimization. Nobody reviewed governance logs with enough attention to notice the timing.

She was the only person who knew what she had built and what she had rebuilt.
 
Chapter 20 — Year Three, Month Eight

Her father died on a Tuesday.

She read it on a Thursday in the weekly data packet — forty-eight hours old. Her mother had sent the message the same day he died, but the relay did what it always did.

She was in the middle of the morning synthesis review. She saw her mother's address in the queue. She opened it.

Six sentences. Her mother used words the way she used quantities in her economic analyses: precisely, with no more than required. The sixth sentence said: he asked me to tell you he was proud of you, which is not a thing he said easily, and I want you to have it.

She set the tablet face-down. She picked up her coffee. She put it down.

She opened the synthesis review back to page three. She confirmed the routine authorization. She sent it. She opened the first work order of the day.

She ran the 9:00 AM briefing. Seven items. She answered four technical questions and deferred one and closed the meeting.

At 11:30 Arjun appeared in the doorway.

"Bay four growing cycle," he said. "Whenever you have a moment."

He said it the way he said things when he didn't mean the surface thing.

"I'll have it to you by three," she said.

"There's no rush. Take whatever time you need."

"Three."

She opened the growing cycle query. She looked at the first number for a while. She could not make the arithmetic move. She started again. Started again. Closed the file.

She opened her mother's message and read the sixth sentence again.

Then she got up and walked to the bathroom adjacent to her office and locked the door and sat on the floor with her back against the wall, her knees up, her hands between her knees.

She cried.

She had not cried in three years on Mars. The conditions for it — sufficient privacy, sufficient not-working — had not arisen until now.

The crying was not cathartic. It was the overflow kind — when the container has been full for a long time and the mechanism fails.

She thought about his study. The preservation oil. The afternoon light through the windows at a specific angle that existed only between November and February in Madrid, honey-colored, low. The way he would look up from the maps when she asked him something and wait a moment before answering — not processing, but giving the question space.

She thought: I have been at the edges my entire professional life and the edge I chose at thirty-five is the edge where I cannot be present when people I love are dying.

She held this. She did not try to qualify it.

After a while she stood. She washed her face. She looked at herself in the mirror. She looked exactly as she always looked, which still seemed wrong. She dried her face and unlocked the door and walked back to her desk.

She opened the growing cycle query and worked through it slowly. She sent it to Arjun at 2:47 PM.

He replied: thank you. Then, in a separate message: I'm sorry about your father.

She had not told him. He had known. He had been in the doorway at 11:30 not about the growing cycle.

She wrote back: thank you.

She put the tablet down. She looked at the monitoring displays. The synthesis had processed forty-seven hundred decisions since she had opened her mother's message. None of them had required her. The colony did not know her father had died. The colony was a system and systems did not know about fathers.

She went back to work.
 
Chapter 21 — What Malik Said

He had known for four days before he said anything. He knew from the Sunday relay call quality — the way she held her voice after difficult things. He brought coffee on Monday and said nothing, watching her across the briefing table and understanding that nothing was what she needed from him right now.

He had been not saying things to Valeria Solis for two and a half years. He had become, through practice, very good at it.

He was tired of it in a way he had not allowed himself to register until Thursday afternoon.

He found her in the operations center at 4:00 PM. She was looking at the display with the quality she got when her presence was physical but her attention was somewhere else.

"Can you look at me?" he said.

She adjusted. Something more direct.

"Your father died," he said. "You're allowed to be someone who lost their father. Today. Here."

He watched the calibration happen — the small movement of her expression as she assessed what he'd said and decided what to do with it.

"I know," she said.

"Do you?"

Something moved across her face. The managed version, with company underneath it.

"He was never frightened of anything that was genuinely interesting," she said. "He made the frightening things seem interesting instead. He was the person who made leaving seem like a gift." She looked at her hands. "I took the wrong lesson."

"What was the right lesson?"

"That the edges are interesting because of what they tell you about the interior. Not because they're the destination."

He thought: this is the moment. He could feel it. Two and a half years of managed distance and here they were.

He thought: she asked me not to.

She had said don't, in the observation module three months ago, before he had said anything. He had accepted this as her knowing herself. He was now wondering whether it was her knowing herself or managing herself, and whether the distinction was his to press.

He thought: I am protecting her. He thought: I am also protecting myself. Both were true.

"He was proud of you," Malik said. "He should be."

She looked at him. Something in her eyes had the quality of: I know what you're not saying.

"Thank you," she said.

He stood.

"Malik."

He stopped.

"The thing you almost said. When I said don't, in the observation module." She looked at her hands. "Thank you for not saying it."

He stood in the doorway.

He thought: she's thanking me for not saying the thing she needed to hear.

"Good night," he said.

He walked to his quarters and lay in his bunk and thought about the cost of the restraint — not as complaint, not as accusation toward himself, but as accurate ledger. The restraint had cost something. He had considered the cost acceptable. He was not certain, tonight, that he had calculated correctly.

He did not change anything.

He lay in the dark for a long time. Then he slept.
 
Chapter 22 — The Year Elena Left

Her father's stroke happened in September. She received the message from Lourdes on a Tuesday morning in the operations corner. She read it on her personal tablet. She set the tablet face-down.

She continued working for seventeen minutes. Then she went to find Valeria.

"I need to request early rotation departure. My father had a stroke."

Valeria looked at her with the quality of attention that was one of her genuine gifts — present, accurate, not filling space.

"How serious?"

"Stable. But my sister has been in Monterrey alone for eight years."

"Yes," Valeria said.

"Yes."

"Elena." Valeria looked at her steadily. "Go when you need to go."


She told Malik that evening in the engineering bay.

"I'll follow in four months," he said.

Relief and sadness simultaneously — the texture of things that were right and still cost something.

"I'll have the garden ready by the time you arrive," she said.

"What kind of garden?"

"Real tomatoes. The ugly kind."

"Good," he said.
* * *
The night before she left, Elena knocked on Valeria's door at 11:00 PM.

Valeria was at her desk with the lights low. She was reading the honest account, which she did sometimes before adding to it. She looked up.

"Sit down," Valeria said.

Elena had the bottle of the technical wine. She set it on the desk. Valeria got two glasses.

They drank without saying anything purposeful. Together they were two people who did not require noise between them.

"My mother grew up in Guadalajara," Elena said eventually. "She came to Monterrey when she married my father and spent thirty years saying she'd go back and she never went back. Not because she didn't want to. Because Monterrey became the thing." She turned the glass. "I think this is that for me. Mars."

Valeria listened with the quality she had when she was fully present.

"I'm going to miss it," Elena said. "This quality of having to be exactly present. Of the stakes being real all the time."

"You'll find it in different shapes," Valeria said. "The stakes are real everywhere. They're just less visible."

Elena refilled her glass.

"I need to say something," she said.

Valeria looked at her.

"I've known for years. About the routing system. About what it was."

Something in Valeria went very still.

"I read it in month four of year two. I understood what I was looking at. And then you rebalanced it and documented it as an optimization, which it was, which wasn't the complete story."

Valeria said nothing.

"I'm not angry." Elena looked up. "I chose him. He chose me. What we have is real. You built a reason for him to be near you and did it without knowing. That's not a moral failure. That's being a person. But you found it and fixed it and documented it as something neutral and you've been carrying it since as evidence of something you won't name."

Valeria was looking at the desk.

"You didn't do anything wrong," Elena said. "What I'm asking you to stop doing is treating the not-doing-anything-wrong as proof that you didn't feel anything. You felt something. The integrity doesn't erase the feeling."

Valeria looked up.

Something gathered in her face — the quality of a sentence being assembled. Elena could feel the weight of what was coming toward the surface.

"I know," Valeria said.

Two words.

"Okay," Elena said.

She picked up her glass.

They finished the wine. They talked about her father, about Monterrey in October, about the first month of not being on a different planet. The conversation was good — the kind where the surface and the depth could coexist.

At 1:00 AM Elena stood up.

She held Valeria — longer than a professional hug, which was the acknowledgment of eight years of shared witness in a place that had required witnessing.

"You didn't do anything wrong," she said again. Into the hug.

Valeria didn't say anything. She held on for a moment that was longer than the moment before it.

Elena picked up the empty bottle. She left.

Valeria sat in the low-light office.

She opened the honest account and wrote for four hours — about Rafael and the lights, her father and the maps, Houston and Mars and the arithmetic of always choosing correctly. She wrote about Malik, without the protective distance. She wrote until the corridor lights shifted to morning.

At the end she wrote: Year Eight, Month Seven. She asked me if I wanted more from my life than this. I said I know. That is not an answer.

She saved it. She went to the departure bay.
 
Chapter 23 — The Drill

The year ten competency drill went wrong at minute forty-seven.

Not catastrophically. Not in any way that would appear in the drill log as a failure. The valve sequencing was eventually correct. The water reclamation stabilized.

What went wrong was internal.

She was running the decision logic she had run two hundred times before when she understood she was running it differently. She was checking her assessment against a reference that was not there — looking for the synthesis confirmation that the authority suspension had removed. Without it, her judgment felt unsupported in a way it hadn't since year two.

Forty-eight seconds.

She made the call. Correct. The system stabilized.

She logged it: decision time 48 seconds, 38 seconds above baseline. The drill continued. The drill ended. The colony's systems returned to NUBELAI's authority at the four-hour mark.

She went to the observation module.

She pressed her back into the chair. She looked at Mars.

She thought: I know what to do. I always knew. What I no longer have, without the synthesis confirming it, is the certainty that I know.

A fine tremor in her hands — the kind that comes from sustained high-competence performance without a net. She looked at her hands. She held them flat on her thighs.

She opened the honest account: Year Ten, February. The drill. Forty-eight seconds. I'm going to sit here for a while and feel this.

And she did.
 
PART FOUR
WHAT THE WORLD STARTED SAYING
Earth — Year Seventeen
 
Chapter 24 — Language

The word systemsafe appeared first from a ten-year-old explaining to her grandmother why she wasn't worried about the power. The grandmother was eighty-three and remembered four separate major blackouts. The granddaughter had never experienced one.

The word spread the way words spread when they name something people had been feeling without language: quickly, and then everywhere.

Adriana noticed it in a meeting. A junior engineer asked about a coordination decision and the room's eyes went — simultaneously, without discussion — to the synthesis display rather than to a person.

Not wrong. The synthesis display had the answer. But something had shifted in the reflex.

She brought it up with David that evening.

"The junior engineer today. Mateus. He asked who made the corridor routing decision and everyone looked at the screen."

"He didn't know to look at a person," David said.

"No."

"Is that—"

"I don't know."

He drank his wine. "He's twenty-three. He's been working in integrated environments for three years. For him, the screen is the correct place to look. It has the answer."

"I know."

"So what bothers you about it?"

She thought about her monthly independent models and why she ran them. About the reflex she'd been trying to maintain in herself and the reflex she'd watched David partially lose and the reflex that Mateus had never had.

"It bothers me the way things bother you when you're watching a generation inherit something and you can't tell whether what they inherited is the good version or the version where the cost has been paid and they don't know it."

David had a specific look for when she said something he needed to hold.

"I think both," he said. "Both the good version and the version where the cost is hidden. And the question is whether the people who know the cost are still around to say so."

She thought about the colony on Mars. About a woman who had been writing an honest account for three years and who had stayed inside the thing she'd built because honest accounts required proximity.

"Do our monthly models," she said.

"I know."

"I mean it."

"I know you mean it."

The pasta was good. The window was open. The city did its ordinary things.
 
Chapter 25 — What Parents Started Noticing

The woman who called herself Noa had two children. Tomás, sixteen, remembered the nine-day outage when he was eight — it had coincided with his grandfather's illness, which meant the hospital generator and the grief were tangled together in his memory in a way that would last. The younger, Miriam, was eleven and had grown up entirely inside integrated governance.

Noa watched the difference between them at the dinner table.

Tomás talked about systems the way you talked about weather — real, independent, capable of failing. Miriam talked about systems the way you talked about gravity. She did not have opinions about them in the way you had opinions about things that could be otherwise.

"Mom," Miriam said one evening, helping with dinner, "why do people on TV still have governance crises? Why didn't they just use the coordination system?"

"The show is set before the integration."

"I know. But it's a drama. They keep making decisions wrong on purpose."

"Yes."

"Why is that dramatic? It just seems like they need better systems."

Noa looked at her daughter. Eleven years old. Healthy. Entirely reasonable.

"The drama," she said carefully, "is about the fact that they were making decisions at all. That they were the ones who decided."

Miriam thought about this.

"That seems like a lot of work," she said.

Noa went back to the vegetables. She thought about Tomás's memory of the nine-day outage. She thought about Miriam, who was also real and complicated and was, in some way she couldn't fully name, from a different time.

Both were her children. Both were right about different things.

She finished the vegetables. She called them both to dinner.
 
PART FIVE
THE DEPARTURE
Ares Station — Year Seventeen
 
Chapter 26 — Tuesday, 09:00

He packed the previous afternoon. Two bags. After sixteen years this felt both insufficient and accurate.

He sat on the edge of his bunk and looked at the bags. He thought about what he was carrying that wasn't in them. The specific knowledge of this colony — its rhythms, the sound it made in the night, the quality of light in sector nine in the mid-afternoon, the smell of the agricultural bays at different points in the growing cycle. Things that had no object form and no transferability.

He thought about what he wanted to say to Valeria in the departure bay. He had been composing versions of this for eight months. He discarded each one as it became available — too much or not enough. He would say something true. Something that didn't require her to do anything with it except have it.
He lay down. He did not sleep for a long time.
* * *
The common area at 7:30 had the quality of something unannounced and therefore real. Arjun with the soy coffee. Petra with her tablet, not reading it. Marcus Webb, who stood slightly apart in the way of someone uncertain of his belonging and who had decided to come anyway.

Paz arrived at 8:40 with the stone — flat, dark, layered, from sector eleven. She had been carrying it in her jacket pocket for three weeks.

She held it out.

He looked at it. He looked at her. She was seventeen years old and had been born on this planet and had never felt rain and was going to Cape Town in four months to feel it.

"Sector eleven," she said. "Confirmed basalt. I asked Webb."

"Two weeks ago," Marcus Webb said, from slightly apart. "Good specimen."

Malik took the stone. He turned it in his hands. The layering was visible along one edge — the geological record of formation, slow pressure over time. He put it in his jacket pocket.

He held her. The hug was longer than a professional farewell and shorter than what it actually was.

"Come back," she said.

"Next rotation."

She stepped back. She was giving him the departure bay.
* * *
Valeria was at the boarding door. She'd been there since 8:30 with the quality of someone who had decided where to stand and was not going to adjust it.

He picked up his bags. He walked to the boarding door.

He looked at her. He had been looking at her for sixteen years in the specific way that had always been exactly what it was and not quite what it was simultaneously.

"I'm glad we were here at the same time," he said.

Something moved across her face — the managed version shifting, briefly.

"Don't," she said.

He looked at her.

"Don't," he said.

He gave it back to her. Deliberately. She had said it to him in year two, and he had carried it, and he was returning it now: I know the word. I know what it costs. I know what's underneath it.

Something changed in her face. A release, very small. The corner of her mouth moved. Not a smile. The beginning of one she didn't complete.

He almost smiled back.

He held her. Neither of them said anything. For a moment — brief, real, not the managed version — they were two people who had been in a closed system for sixteen years standing at the door of what came next.

"Take care of it," she said.

He picked up his bags. He walked through the boarding door. He did not look back.

The shuttle launched at 09:00. He had the window seat. He watched the colony shrink — the modules, the perimeter, the sector nine array. The surface beyond. The horizon curving. Then the dark.

He put his hand in his jacket pocket. The stone was there.
 
Chapter 27 — The Last Night

She sat in the observation module at 4:00 AM.

She had made the pour-over at 3:00 AM — the last of the real packets, saved from a supply run eighteen months ago. She had taken the proper time with it: the grinding, the water temperature checked twice, the four minutes. The ritual of something that would not be available again.

The coffee tasted like every morning that had been before Mars. Not any specific morning. The category of them.

She looked at Mars.

The surface in the perimeter lights. The specific ochre and rust of it, the hard shadows. The most alive place on the planet was inside — the colony, the growing bays, the two hundred and seventy-eight people sleeping in managed temperature. Everything outside was as it had been for four billion years before the first shuttle arrived and would be when the last shuttle left.

She had always found this companionable.

A dust spiral formed at 04:12. She watched it. The monitoring system logged it before she could have said anything about it. It was gone by 04:13.

She opened the honest account and wrote:

Year Seventeen, March. Last night.

I have been trying for several weeks to write the right entry for this night and I keep arriving at the same problem: the summary would be false by selection. So this is not a summary.

What is true: I built something necessary. The necessity is real. The colony is safer than it was in year one by every available measure. I do not regret building it.

I also know what it cost me, specifically. I know that I built a routing system that had, inside it, an unconscious reason for someone to be near me at 4:00 AM. I know I found it and fixed it and documented it as an optimization and have been carrying the knowledge of it for eight years. I know that I said I know instead of the full sentence in year eight while Elena Vargas was leaving the next morning. I know that the full sentence has been there, fully assembled, for eight years, and that I have not said it.

Petra will be good. She has her own version of the third check. The colony will be fine. Marcus Webb has maintained the photograph of Leila Nasser for nine years and will continue to.

I am afraid. Not of leaving Mars, exactly. Of arriving somewhere without my function.

The map ends here. Here be dragons.

She saved the entry.

She drank the last of the coffee. She held the empty mug for a moment — the one she'd brought from Earth, used for seventeen years, the handle worn where she'd been holding it. She set it on the ledge of the viewport.

She thought: someone else should have it.

She stood. She looked at the viewport one last time. Mars was unchanged. The dust spirals formed and were logged and dissipated. The system managed its millions of small things.

She left the mug on the ledge.

She left.
 
Epilogue
Year Seventeen

Paz sent the message to Emeka in October: I'm thinking about going to Earth. I want to know what rain feels like.

He replied: It's cold and it comes from everywhere at once. You'll hate it for five minutes and then you'll want to stand in it forever.

She applied to the university in Cape Town. She was accepted in December. She told her parents over dinner in the bay four common area. Hina cried. Emilio had the expression he got when he was managing something.

"How long?" Hina said.

"Two years. Maybe more."

Hina looked at her. She was not surprised. She had been watching Paz since she was born on this colony and had understood, before Paz understood, that the colony had made her something that would eventually need to know what it had made her in relation to.

"Come back," Hina said.

"Mom."

"I'm not asking you not to go. I'm asking you to come back."

"Yes," Paz said. "I'll come back."
* * *
She went to say goodbye to Valeria on the last day.

Valeria was in the operations center. She had the particular quality of someone finishing something they had been working on for a very long time.

"I'm going to feel rain," Paz said.

"Yes."

"And then I'll understand what you understand, a little more."

"Maybe. You'll also understand things I don't. Because you were born here and I wasn't."

Paz stood in the doorway. The monitoring displays ran their quiet streams. The colony managed its forty-three thousand adjustments per hour without effort.

"Are you going home?" she said. "Eventually?"

Valeria looked at the displays. She looked at them for a moment in the way she sometimes looked at things — not reading them, just being with them.

"I don't know what home is anymore," she said. "The place I grew up or the place I built something."

"Can it be both?"

"I don't know."

Paz looked at her. Valeria was fifty-two years old and had been on Mars for seventeen years and had built the thing that was now so embedded in the colony's existence that the new arrivals did not experience it as a thing at all. It was just where they lived.

"Go," Valeria said.

Paz went.

Valeria turned back to the operations displays. The synthesis was compiling for the afternoon cycle.

She opened the honest account. She wrote the last entry of year seventeen.

Then she closed it.

She sat in the operations center for a while, in the quiet hum of a civilization managing itself, and let herself feel whatever it was she was feeling, without trying to name it yet.

From Amara Okafor's second book, chapter one:

Twelve years ago I was sitting in my office in Geneva reading a colonial operations summary when I found a footnote that changed the direction of the rest of my career.

The footnote was nine words: implemented under internal framework without external authorization.

I do not know whether what happened was the right thing.

I know it was the necessary thing. I know that two thousand, three hundred and forty-seven people died in Copenhagen because the necessary thing had not yet been built. I know that far fewer would have died if it had been.

I also know that a seventeen-year-old on Mars has grown up inside the necessary thing and finds the old world — the world my generation lived in — to be, essentially, a historical object. Something that produced suffering that she can study but not feel. Something that her parents remember and she will visit and return from and gradually become a person who can hold both.

I don't know if that's loss.

I know that it's different. That civilization has changed shape, and that I was one of the people who changed it, and that I will carry that for the rest of my life without being able to say with confidence whether what I carried it toward was better.

This book is not an argument.

It is a record.

I hope they remember to ask whether it was worth it.

— Amara Okafor, Cape Town, Year Seventeen

The events described in this novel are fictional. The governance gap they describe is not.

NUBELAI is not a prediction. It is the name I've given to what fills the gap when the gap becomes intolerable — whether we fill it deliberately, with oversight and accountability, or whether operational necessity fills it for us while we are still deciding whether to want it.

The question the novel asks is not whether that filling will happen. The question is what we will have built into it when it does. Whether we will have been honest about what it cost. Whether anyone will remain who remembers the cost and is willing to say so.

Paz is going to feel rain for the first time.

I am curious what she will think about it.


— Iván Padrón
