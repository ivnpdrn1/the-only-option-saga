The core diagnosis the brief makes is correct. The novel was showing transition among people who were capable of adaptation — Valeria, Malik, Elena, Arjun, Amara, Okafor. These people are thoughtful, observant, and largely functional. The world outside the colony and outside the governance corridors was not sufficiently present. Resistance, cost, and the specifically human experience of becoming unnecessary — these needed to appear in the flesh, not just in abstract reference.

Seven additions:
1. Brisbane (Chapter 9) — The sign that says THEY BUILT IT AND LEFT
After Chapter 9's ending, Valeria reads a news item in the weekly relay packet about forty-three logistics workers in Brisbane who disabled their regional coordination nodes. The incident is clinical on its surface: eight hours of manual scheduling, misdirected cargo, a hospital in Toowoomba that runs out of a blood product. But the detail that matters is the photograph: three protest signs. The first two are expected — WE REMEMBER HOW, NUBELAI DOES NOT SPEAK FOR US. The third, held by a woman not looking at the camera: THEY BUILT IT AND LEFT.
That sign is aimed at Valeria. She doesn't name this. She reads the item three times. She tells nobody. She goes to the common area and eats alone. The resistance is not heroic. The resistance is not wrong. The system made it worse. She knows all of this simultaneously.
2. Amara's political pressure (Chapter 15)
Before Amara makes her sandwich and reads the archive, she has already calculated the calls she will receive after scheduling the review session. Commissioner Reinholt. The French sovereignty contingent. The US caucus message warning that convening a review implies something is wrong. None of them will be wrong, exactly. None of them will be about the footnote. This is how institutional governance actually functions — good-faith actors protecting frameworks that are already failing, for reasons that are also good-faith. The novel needed this texture: the difficulty of moving is not stubbornness or corruption but the accumulated weight of previous correct decisions.
3. Ingrid Solberg and Astrid (Chapter 16)

4. Harjit Singh Brar (Interlude)
Before the systemsafe section, the Interlude now opens with a man in Leeds who ran his father's precision manufacturing business for thirty-one years. When integration arrived, he did everything right — attended every meeting, filed the exemption request, reviewed the data. The data was right. He knew it was right. He shut the business down anyway at sixty-one, not because he was failing but because he could not identify a single decision the system was making that he would have made differently, and he found this unbearable in a way he had no language for. His son called him irrational. He agreed. He went home and didn't know what to do with his hands.
He is not on a sign. He is not in any record. He grows vegetables in a garden too large for two people. He visits on Sundays. They talk about other things.
This is the cost that doesn't appear in reports: the person who sees the system is right and cannot survive the rightness of it.
